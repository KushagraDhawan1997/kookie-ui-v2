An interruption stops whatever someone was doing. There are five levels of it here, and the
rule is to take the cheapest one that does the job.

## The five levels

From cheapest to most expensive:

- **Inline.** Change the thing where it stands: an error under the field that caused it, a
  switch that takes effect immediately, a row that expands. Nothing covers anything and nobody
  loses their place.
- **A menu.** Use `Menu` for a short list of actions and `Select` for a list of values. It
  covers a small rectangle and closes on Escape, on an outside press, or when you pick
  something. Everything underneath stays readable.
- **A popover.** `Popover` covers a small rectangle like a menu, and holds whatever you put in
  it: a filter form, a date picker, a short piece of detail. **The page stays live.** Nothing
  dims, focus is not trapped, and you can keep working around it. That is the whole line between
  this and a dialog, and it is why a small form does not need one.
- **A dialog.** `Dialog` dims the whole app and traps focus. Use it for work that needs room
  away from the page.
- **An alert.** `AlertDialog` covers the app the same way, and does not let anyone leave without
  answering.

`Tooltip` is not on this list, because it interrupts nothing and carries nothing. It restates
the name a control already announces, to a pointer resting on it. Anything that appears only in
a tooltip is information a keyboard and a touch user never get.

`Sheet` belongs on this list and has not shipped yet. Use a `Dialog` until it does, and keep
any stand-in you build in one file so there is one place to change on the day the real thing
arrives.

## Choosing between a popover and a dialog

Both cover a piece of the screen and hold content you wrote. The question is not how much
content there is, but whether the rest of the app should keep working.

A popover leaves it working. The page is live behind it, so you can read the row it came from,
scroll, or click elsewhere to dismiss it. Reach for it when the panel is *about* something on
the page: filters over a list, a picker beside the field it fills, detail on the thing you
pressed.

A dialog stops the app. It dims everything and traps focus, which is right when the panel IS
the task and the page behind it is nothing but context. Reach for it when leaving mid-way would
lose work, or when the content needs more room than a panel beside a trigger can have.

If you are wrapping a small form in a dialog because a dialog is the component you know, that
is the popover case.

## Choosing between a dialog and an alert

The two panels look nearly identical and are two different things.

A dialog holds your work. You opened it, it contains a form or a picker or a document, and you
can leave it the way you came in.

An alert stops you with a question. It may have no trigger at all, because something raised it
when a decision had to happen before anything else could.

**A panel that needs any control beyond its two buttons is a Dialog.** A type-to-confirm field,
a checkbox or a form of any size each crosses that line, because the panel is now holding work
rather than a choice.

## An alert owns its own content

An alert's contents are fixed: a title, a description, a cancel button and an action button, in
that order, laid out by the component.

```tsx
<AlertDialog>
  <AlertDialogTrigger render={<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>} />
  <AlertDialogContent>
    <AlertDialogTitle>Delete this project?</AlertDialogTitle>
    <AlertDialogDescription>
      Its documents and their history go with it. This cannot be undone.
    </AlertDialogDescription>
    <AlertDialogCancel>Keep it</AlertDialogCancel>
    <AlertDialogAction tone="destructive">Delete project</AlertDialogAction>
  </AlertDialogContent>
</AlertDialog>
```

`size` on an alert sets everything: the box, the corner, the padding, both text sizes and both
buttons. The system wrote all of that content, so the system may size it.

`size` on a `Dialog` sets the box and the two parts the system owns, `DialogTitle` and
`DialogDescription`. It never touches the content you wrote, because nothing here sizes text it
does not own.

Write cancel first. That order decides focus: cancel reads first, sits at the start, flips
correctly for a right-to-left language at no cost, and takes focus as the first thing you can
tab to. Document order picks the safe answer for you.

## Why a dialog casts no shadow

The dialog panel casts no shadow of its own. Anything floating has to show what it covers, and
a dialog does that by darkening the entire window. A shadow under the panel would say the same
thing a second time.

The panel keeps the ordinary card treatment of whichever world it is in — raised in a raised
theme, flat in a flat one.

The dimming is also why the panel barely moves when it opens. The change has already been
announced.

Under high contrast, and for anyone who has asked for reduced transparency, the dimming gives
one answer: more colour and no blur.

## Focus and getting out

Both panels trap focus while open and return it where it came from when they close, so a
keyboard never falls out of the layer and never lands nowhere afterwards.

Neither takes an initial-focus prop. That is a refusal rather than a gap: what the panel
contains decides where focus should land, which is why the alert puts cancel first in its own
tab order.

A dialog closes on Escape, on an outside press, and on any `DialogClose` you place. An alert
closes on Escape and on its two buttons only. **An alert does not close on an outside press**,
because a stray click must not answer a question about deleting something.

Escape stays, though. A keyboard "not now" is cancel by another route, and removing it would
shut a keyboard user inside a panel they could only leave by committing to one of the answers.

There is no built-in close cross in the corner, for the same reason `Card` has no header slot:
that is layout rather than wiring. `DialogClose` puts a real button wherever your layout wants
one. Someone using a screen reader on a touch device needs that button to leave a trapped
panel.

## Name the consequence

Write what will happen. "Delete project" beats "OK". "Keep it" beats "Cancel" whenever the safe
answer has a name. The description carries the consequence and its scope in as few words as
will hold them.

Put the tone on the action button, never on the panel. The alert itself stays neutral, and the
destructive act is the one red thing in it.

An alert holds exactly one action button, so a loud button is the right default there. The
anatomy holds the one-loud-action rule for you, and nobody has to remember it.

## Anything that covers must show its edge

Covering something is information, so whatever covers has to show where it ends. In a raised
theme a menu casts a shadow. In a flat theme it draws a thin line instead, because a flat theme
draws every boundary with a line. A dialog does it with the dimmed background.

None of this is a decision you make at the call site. No component here takes a shadow. Knowing
the rule exists stops you looking for one.
