Interrupting someone is expensive, and the cost is not the pixels — it is that whatever they
were doing stops. This system ships four levels of interruption, and the rule is to take the
lowest one that does the job. Reaching for a dialog because a dialog is easier to write than an
inline edit is the most common way an interface becomes tiring to use.

## The ladder of interruption

Four rungs, cheapest first.

- **Inline.** Change the thing in place. An error under the field that caused it, a `Switch`
  that takes effect on the spot, a row that opens. Nothing covers anything, and the person keeps
  their place.
- **A menu.** `Menu` for a short list of actions, `Select` for a list of values. It covers a
  small rectangle, it dismisses on Escape, on an outside press and on choosing, and it leaves
  the page underneath legible.
- **A dialog.** `Dialog` puts a scrim over the whole app, traps focus, and hosts work that
  genuinely needs room away from the page.
- **An alert.** `AlertDialog` covers the same way and will not let you leave without answering.

`Popover`, `Tooltip` and `Sheet` are specified on this ladder and have not shipped. Until they
do, a `Menu` covers the anchored case and a `Dialog` covers the rest. If you build a stand-in,
keep it in one file: the day the real component lands, you want one place to change rather than
a habit spread across a codebase.

## A dialog is summoned; an alert arrives

The pair looks identical and is two kinds of thing. **A dialog is summoned to host your work.**
You opened it, it holds a form or a picker or a document, and you can leave it the way you came.
**An alert comes at you and stops you with a question.** It may not even have a trigger: it is
raised because something needs deciding before anything else happens.

The boundary is one sentence. **If it needs any control beyond its two buttons, it is a
Dialog.** A type-to-confirm field, a checkbox, a form of any size — any one of them and the
thing has become work rather than a choice.

That is also why the alert's content is closed. Title, description, cancel, action, in that
order, laid out by the component. Its `size` prices everything — the box, the corner, the
padding, both type steps and both buttons — because the system owns all of that content. A
`Dialog`'s `size` stops at the box, because a dialog holds content the system did not write, and
no surface in this system sizes type it does not own.

```tsx
<AlertDialog>
  <AlertDialogTrigger render={<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>} />
  <AlertDialogContent>
    <AlertDialogTitle>Delete this project?</AlertDialogTitle>
    <AlertDialogDescription>
      Its documents and their history go with it. This cannot be undone.
    </AlertDialogDescription>
    <AlertDialogCancel>Keep it</AlertDialogCancel>
    <AlertDialogAction tone="destructive">Delete project</AlertDialogAction>
  </AlertDialogContent>
</AlertDialog>
```

Cancel is written first, and that ordering *is* the focus mechanism: it reads first, it sits at
the start, it flips for right-to-left at no cost, and it takes initial focus because it is the
first thing that can be tabbed to. The safest action is chosen by document order rather than by
machinery pointed at it.

## The scrim is the separation

The panel casts nothing of its own. A floating layer owes a statement of what it covers, and a
dialog states it with the entire viewport going dark — a shadow underneath would be saying the
same thing twice. What the panel keeps is the world's ordinary surface chrome, the same one a
`Card` takes: lifted in an app with light in it, flat in a flat one.

That is also what buys the panel the right to move almost not at all when it arrives. The room
went dark; the thing on top of it does not need to fly in as well.

Under `contrast="high"`, and for a person who has asked their system for reduced transparency,
the scrim gets the same answer: more pigment, no defocus.

## Focus, dismissal, and the one refusal

Both panels trap focus while they are open and return it where it came from when they close, so
a keyboard never falls out of the layer and never lands nowhere afterwards. Neither takes an
initial-focus prop, and that is a refusal rather than a gap: where focus should land is decided
by what the panel holds, which is why the alert puts Cancel first in its own tab order instead
of pointing machinery at it.

A dialog closes on Escape, on an outside press, and on any `DialogClose` you place. An alert
closes on Escape and on its two buttons, and **not** on an outside press: a stray click must not
be able to answer a question about deleting something.

Escape stays, and the reason is worth stating. A keyboard "not now" is Cancel by another route —
the same decision, reached by the same person, through the input they happen to be using.
Removing it would leave a keyboard user shut inside a panel they can only leave by committing to
one of its answers, which is not a safer design, only a stricter one.

There is no corner ✕ as anatomy, for the reason `Card` has no header slot: it is layout, not
wiring. `DialogClose` puts a real `Button` wherever the composition wants one, which is also
what a screen-reader user on a touch device needs in order to get out of a trapped panel.

## Name the consequence, not the mechanism

Confirmation copy says what will happen. "Delete project" beats "OK"; "Keep it" beats "Cancel"
whenever the safe answer has a name of its own. The description carries the consequence and its
scope in as few words as will hold them, and it is the one place in a destructive flow where an
extra clause is worth the reading.

The tone goes on the Action, never on the panel. The alert stays neutral and the destructive act
is the single red thing inside it, which is figure and ground at the smallest scale this system
works at. An alert has exactly one Action, so the loud rung is legal here as a default — the
one-focal-point rule is held by the component's anatomy instead of by anyone remembering it.

## Every covering layer states its coverage

Overlap is information rather than expression, so anything that covers has to bound itself. In
an app with light in it a menu casts the floating chrome; in a flat app it draws its hairline
instead, because a flat world's boundary vocabulary is the line. A dialog states it with the
scrim. None of that is a call-site decision — no component in this system takes a shadow — but
knowing that the rule exists is what stops anyone reaching for one.
