An interruption stops the work the person was doing. This system ships four levels of
interruption. Take the lowest level that does the job.

## The four levels of interruption

The list runs from the cheapest level to the most expensive one.

- **Inline.** Change the thing in place. An error under the field that caused it, a `Switch`
  that takes effect at once, and a row that opens are the cases. Nothing covers anything, and
  the person keeps their place.
- **A menu.** Use `Menu` for a short list of actions. Use `Select` for a list of values. A menu
  covers a small rectangle. It closes on Escape, on an outside press, and on a choice. The page
  below it stays readable.
- **A dialog.** `Dialog` puts a scrim over the whole app. It traps focus. It holds work that
  needs room away from the page.
- **An alert.** `AlertDialog` covers the app the same way. It does not let the person leave
  without an answer.

The specification names `Popover`, `Tooltip` and `Sheet` on this ladder, and none of the three
has shipped. Use a `Menu` for the anchored case until they do. Use a `Dialog` for the rest. Keep
any stand-in you build in one file, so you change one place on the day the real component lands.

## Choose between a dialog and an alert

The two panels render almost the same and they are two kinds of thing.

A dialog hosts your work. You opened it. It holds a form, a picker or a document. You can leave
it the way you came in.

An alert stops you with a question. It may have no trigger at all. Something raised it because a
decision has to happen before anything else does.

One sentence sets the boundary. **A panel that needs any control beyond its two buttons is a
`Dialog`.** A type-to-confirm field, a checkbox and a form of any size each cross that line. The
panel then holds work rather than a choice.

## The alert dialog owns its content

The content of an alert is closed. It holds a title, a description, a cancel button and an action
button, in that order. The component lays them out.

```tsx
<AlertDialog>
  <AlertDialogTrigger render={<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>} />
  <AlertDialogContent>
    <AlertDialogTitle>Delete this project?</AlertDialogTitle>
    <AlertDialogDescription>
      Its documents and their history go with it. This cannot be undone.
    </AlertDialogDescription>
    <AlertDialogCancel>Keep it</AlertDialogCancel>
    <AlertDialogAction tone="destructive">Delete project</AlertDialogAction>
  </AlertDialogContent>
</AlertDialog>
```

`size` on an alert prices everything: the box, the corner, the padding, both type steps and both
buttons. The system owns all of that content, so the system may size it. `size` on a `Dialog`
stops at the box. A dialog holds content the system did not write, and no surface in this system
sizes type it does not own.

Write cancel first. That order is the focus mechanism. Cancel reads first and it sits at the
start. It flips for a right-to-left language at no cost. It takes initial focus as the first
tabbable element. Document order chooses the safest action.

## The scrim is the separation

The dialog panel casts no shadow of its own. Every floating layer states what it covers, and a
dialog states it with the whole viewport going dark. A shadow under the panel would state the
same thing twice.

The panel keeps the ordinary surface chrome, the same chrome a `Card` takes. It is lifted in an
elevated theme and flat in a flat one.

The scrim is also what lets the panel move almost not at all when it opens. The scrim already
stated the change.

Under `contrast="high"`, and for a person who asks the operating system for reduced transparency,
the scrim gives one answer: more pigment and no defocus.

## Focus and dismissal

Both panels trap focus while they are open. Both return focus where it came from when they close.
A keyboard never falls out of the layer, and it never lands nowhere afterwards.

Neither panel takes an initial-focus prop. That is a refusal, not a gap. What the panel holds
decides where focus lands, which is why the alert puts cancel first in its own tab order.

A dialog closes on Escape, on an outside press, and on any `DialogClose` you place. An alert
closes on Escape and on its two buttons. An alert does **not** close on an outside press. A stray
press must not answer a question about deleting something.

Escape stays on the alert. A keyboard "not now" is cancel by another route. Removing Escape would
shut a keyboard user inside a panel they can only leave by committing to one of its answers.

There is no corner close glyph as anatomy, for the reason `Card` has no header slot. It is
layout, not wiring. `DialogClose` places a real `Button` wherever the composition wants one. A
screen reader user on a touch device needs that button to leave a trapped panel.

## Name the consequence

Write what will happen. "Delete project" is better than "OK". "Keep it" is better than "Cancel"
whenever the safe answer has a name. The description carries the consequence and its scope in as
few words as will hold them.

Put the tone on the action button, never on the panel. The alert stays neutral, and the
destructive act is the one red thing inside it.

An alert holds exactly one action button, so the loud rung is legal here as a default. The
anatomy holds the one-focal-action rule. Nobody has to remember it.

## Every covering layer states its coverage

Overlap is information, so anything that covers has to state its own boundary. A menu casts the
floating chrome in an elevated theme. A menu draws its hairline in a flat theme, because a flat
theme states a boundary with a line. A dialog states it with the scrim.

None of this is a call-site decision. No component in this system takes a shadow. Knowing that
the rule exists is what stops a reader reaching for one.
