Kookie gives you several ways to put something in front of a person: change it inline, open a
`Menu`, open a `Popover`, open a `Dialog` or a `Sheet`, or show an `AlertDialog`. Each one
interrupts more than the one before it. Pick the one that interrupts the least and still does
the job.

## From least to most interrupting

- **Inline.** Change the thing where it is: an error under the field that caused it, a switch
  that takes effect straight away, a row that expands. Nothing is covered, and nobody loses their
  place.
- **A menu.** Use `Menu` for a short list of actions and `Select` for a list of values. A menu
  covers a small area next to its trigger and closes on Escape, on a press outside it, or when
  you choose an item. Everything around it stays readable.
- **A popover.** A `Popover` also covers a small area next to its trigger, but it holds whatever
  you put in it, such as a filter form, a date picker or a few lines of detail. **The page stays
  live.** Nothing dims, focus isn't trapped, and a person can keep working around it.
- **A dialog or a sheet.** A `Dialog` dims the whole app and traps focus until it closes. Use it
  for work that needs room away from the page. A `Sheet` behaves the same way, but it stands against
  the bottom or the side of the window instead of sitting in the middle, and a person can swipe
  it away on a touch screen.
- **An alert.** An `AlertDialog` covers the app like a dialog, and a person can't leave it
  without answering.

`Tooltip` isn't on this list, because it interrupts nothing and holds nothing of its own. It
shows the name of a control when a pointer rests on it, and screen readers never hear it. Touch
screens have no hover, so a touch user never sees it either. Never put information in a tooltip
that isn't also somewhere else.

## Choosing between a popover and a dialog

Both hold content you wrote, so the question isn't how much content there is. The question is
whether the rest of the app needs to keep working while the panel is open.

With a popover, it does. The page behind it stays live: a person can read the row that opened
it, scroll, or click somewhere else to close it. Use a popover when the panel is about something
on the page, such as filters over a list, a picker next to the field it fills, or details about
the item that was pressed.

A dialog stops the app. It dims everything and traps focus, which is right when the panel is the
task and the page behind it is only context. Use a dialog when someone would lose work by leaving
halfway, or when the content needs more room than a panel next to a button can give it.

If you're reaching for a dialog only because it's the component you know, and the content is a
small form, use a popover instead.

## Choosing between a dialog and an alert

The two panels look almost the same, but they do different jobs.

A dialog holds work that a person opened on purpose: a form, a picker or a document. They can
leave it the same way they came in.

An alert stops a person with a question. Something opened it because a decision has to be made
before anything else can happen, and it may have no trigger at all.

One rule separates them: **if the panel needs any control besides its two buttons, it's a
`Dialog`.** A type-to-confirm field, a checkbox or a form of any size turns the panel into work
rather than a choice.

## Writing an alert

An alert always has the same four parts, in the same order: a title, a description, a cancel
button and an action button. Put them directly inside `AlertDialogContent`, and the component
lays them out for you.

```tsx
<AlertDialog>
  <AlertDialogTrigger render={<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>} />
  <AlertDialogContent>
    <AlertDialogTitle>Delete this project?</AlertDialogTitle>
    <AlertDialogDescription>
      Its documents and their history go with it. This cannot be undone.
    </AlertDialogDescription>
    <AlertDialogCancel>Keep it</AlertDialogCancel>
    <AlertDialogAction tone="destructive">Delete project</AlertDialogAction>
  </AlertDialogContent>
</AlertDialog>
```

Write the cancel button first. That order puts it at the start of the row, flips it correctly in
right-to-left languages, and gives it focus when the alert opens, because focus goes to the first
button.

`AlertDialogAction` is loud by default, because an alert has exactly one action. Put the tone on
the action button, never on the panel, so the destructive button is the only red thing in the
alert.

## How size works on each panel

On an `AlertDialog`, `size` sets everything: the width, the padding, the corner, both text sizes
and both buttons. The system writes all of that content, so it sets all of its sizes.

On a `Dialog`, `size` sets the width, the padding and the corner, and the text size of
`DialogTitle` and `DialogDescription`. It doesn't change anything you put in the dialog yourself,
so set the size on your own text and controls. `Popover` works the same way, with
`PopoverTitle` and `PopoverDescription`.

## Name the consequence

Label buttons with what will happen. "Delete project" is clearer than "OK", and when the safe
answer has a natural name, "Keep it" can be clearer than "Cancel". Use the description to say
what the action affects, in as few words as you can.

## Focus and closing

A dialog, a sheet and an alert all trap focus while they're open, and return focus to where it
came from when they close. Keyboard focus never escapes the panel, and it always has somewhere to
go afterwards. None of them has a prop for initial focus: focus usually goes to the first control in
the panel, which is why the order you write things in matters.

A dialog closes on Escape, on a press outside it, and on any `DialogClose` you add. An alert
closes on Escape and on its two buttons. **An alert doesn't close on a press outside it**,
because an accidental click shouldn't answer a question about deleting something. Escape still
works, and it does the same thing as the cancel button, so a keyboard user always has a way out.

There's no close button in the dialog's corner. Place a `DialogClose` wherever your layout needs
one, usually as a `Button` in the row of actions. Keep one in every dialog, because a screen
reader user on a touch device needs a button to leave a panel that traps focus.

## Showing where a covering panel ends

Anything that covers part of the screen has to show where it ends, and the theme handles that for
you. In an elevated theme a menu or popover casts a shadow, and in a flat theme it draws a thin
line around its edge instead.

A dialog shows its edge by dimming the whole window behind it, so it adds no shadow of its own.
The panel looks like an ordinary card: raised in an elevated theme and flat in a flat one. In
high contrast mode, or when a person asks the system for reduced transparency, the dimmed
background becomes darker and loses its blur.

No component has a shadow prop, so you never make this decision in your code.
