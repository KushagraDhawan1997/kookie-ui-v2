Kookie prioritises correctness over ease of adoption. While some interface questions have
measurable answers, others rely on design judgment. Kookie applies measurements automatically
and centralises every decision to ensure your screens remain consistent.

## Measured limits

Decades of research inform interface design. Where a standard measurement exists, Kookie applies
it automatically, and no prop can override the result.

- **Fitts's law (1954):** Predicts the time required to move a pointer to a target. This time
  increases as the target gets smaller or further away.
- **Tap targets:** A fingertip is about 10 millimetres across, which informs the 44-point
  minimum on Apple platforms and the 48-pixel minimum on Android. WCAG 2.2 sets a lower baseline
  of 24 pixels.
- **Gestalt principles (1923):** Describe how the human eye groups visual elements before
  reading them. Because distance carries meaning, the space between two elements changes how
  they are interpreted.
- **Contrast:** Both WCAG and APCA measure whether text is legible against its background
  colour.

## How limits shape components

These measurements define the default sizes throughout the system, adapting automatically across
devices from desktops to phones.

- **Touch screens:** A `Theme` detects whether the pointer is a mouse or a finger. For touch
  inputs, controls like checkboxes and radio buttons scale up to their touch size, with default
  controls reaching 44 pixels.
- **Checkboxes:** A checkbox remains visually small to sit beside a single line of text, but its
  tappable area extends to the size of a standard `Button`. This ensures usability without
  disrupting the surrounding layout.
- **Typography on mobile:** Because you hold a phone closer to your eyes than a desktop screen,
  small text sizes are increased on handheld devices. Conversely, large display sizes are
  reduced on narrow windows because fewer characters fit on a single line.
- **Text fields:** On touch screens, text fields have a minimum font size of 16 pixels. This
  prevents Safari on iOS from automatically zooming the page when the field is focused.
- **Round controls:** When you set `radius="full"`, the corners curve inward beside the first
  and last letters. These controls require wider side padding than those with smaller corner
  radii.

## Judgments

Beyond measured limits, design decisions become judgments. This category includes corner radius,
the saturation of a glass panel, accent hues, and the internal spacing of a card.

Kookie strictly separates these two categories. Measured limits are enforced and cannot be
overridden. Judgments are recorded as single values, meaning one edit updates the value
everywhere. Kookie never presents a judgment as a measurement, avoiding the attribution of false
precision to a chosen value.

## Each decision has one place

An inconsistent system conceals its cost initially. Your first and fifth screens may be correct,
but by the twentieth screen, the same question has been answered differently across a dozen
files, and no screen reflects the decisions of the others.

Kookie answers each question once, and your screens read that answer rather than restating it.
This is why components accept a `tone` and an `emphasis` instead of a colour, why there is no
margin prop, and why the system lacks a `variant` prop.

## Components are organised by what they do

Kookie classifies components by function rather than appearance. Two components can look
identical but perform different jobs. Kookie separates these components, giving each its own
name and props.

Tabs and SegmentedControl demonstrate this distinction. Both render a row of segments, but Tabs
moves you between views and replaces the content below, while a SegmentedControl sets a value
and leaves the surrounding content in place.

These controls also announce themselves differently to screen readers. Tabs reports a tab list
and names each panel, whereas a SegmentedControl reports a radio group where arrow keys change
the value. Neither component replicates the other's behaviour, so no prop can convert one into
the other.

To distinguish between two similar components, consider their function independently. You can
switch views without setting a value, and you can set a value without switching views. These are
two separate operations, not one operation with two appearances. The
[Vocabulary](/start/vocabulary) chapter lists other pairs that share an appearance.

## Components and patterns

A component contains what it owns, while a pattern coordinates what it does not contain. A
button owns its label, icon, and padding, making it a component. An app frame coordinates the
header, sidebar, content, and window width, making it a pattern. You assemble patterns from
components; the [Navigation](/patterns/navigation) chapter demonstrates how to assemble one for
each window size.

The children of a component never configure their parent, which makes the two easy to
distinguish. If a child must change its parent, such as a sidebar setting the page margin, the
result is a pattern. You should build this pattern in your application rather than expecting a
single component to provide it.

## The trade-off

A system that centralises decisions offers fewer overrides than you might expect. When a design
does not fit, no `variant` prop is available, and Kookie will refuse the input. Each refusal
states a reason and an alternative, and each component page lists its own refusals. The only
escape hatch is `style`, which remains highly visible in code reviews.

In return, the system remains consistent as your application grows, enforcing its decisions
rather than relying on developer memory.
