Kookie organises its components by what they do, rather than by how they look. Two components
can look almost identical and still do different jobs, and when that happens the system keeps
them separate, each with its own name and its own props. Tabs and SegmentedControl are the
clearest example.

## Behaviour comes first

A pattern is a recurring structure: a context, a problem, and the shape of the solution. A
component is a pattern packaged for reuse. A name is the label on the package.

Kookie decides in that order: the pattern first, then the component, then the name. A
component that starts from a name and a picture gets its behaviour last, one prop at a time.

## Two components that look alike

Tabs and SegmentedControl both draw a row of segments, and at a glance they're hard to tell
apart. They do different jobs, however. Tabs moves you between views, so it replaces whatever
sits under it. SegmentedControl sets a value in place, so what sits under it stays and only its
contents change.

The two controls above look almost the same, and they announce themselves differently. Tabs
announces itself to a screen reader as a tab list and gives each panel a name. SegmentedControl
announces itself as a radio group, so a screen reader says "3 of 3 selected" and the arrow keys
change the value. Neither component can produce the other's behaviour.

To tell two look-alikes apart, ask what each one does when the other is absent. You can switch
views without setting a value, and you can set a value without switching views. That makes them
two operations rather than one operation with two appearances. So no `variant` prop turns one
into the other.

## Components and patterns

A component contains the things it owns. A pattern coordinates things it doesn't contain. A
button owns its label, its icon, and its padding, so it's a component. An app frame has to
coordinate the header, the sidebar, the content, and the window width, so it's a pattern. You
build a pattern from components. The [Navigation](/patterns/navigation) chapter shows how to build one
for each window size.

The two are easy to tell apart in practice, because a component's children never configure
their parent. If a child has to reach upward and change its parent, like a sidebar setting the
page's margin, you're looking at a pattern. Build it in your app rather than asking one
component to hold it.

## Measurements and judgments

Not every design question is a matter of taste. Some of them have been measured.

- Fitts's law, from 1954, predicts how long it takes to move a pointer to a target, from the
  distance and the size of that target.
- A fingertip is about 10 millimetres across, which is where every platform's 44-point
  minimum tap target comes from.
- The Gestalt principles, from 1923, describe how your eye groups things before you read them.
- WCAG and APCA both measure whether text is readable against its background.

Past the measured limits, a decision is a judgment: the exact corner radius, how saturated a
glass panel should be, or which hue the accent takes.

The system keeps the two apart. Where something has been measured, the system enforces it, and
no prop can undo it. Where something is a judgment, it's written down as a single value, and
changing it is a one-line edit. The system doesn't present judgments as measurements.

## Consistency

An inconsistent system doesn't show its cost right away. The first screen is fine, and so is
the fifth. By the twentieth, the same questions have been answered a dozen times in a dozen
places, and no screen can see what the others decided.

Kookie avoids that by deciding once. Each decision lives in one place, and every screen reads
it.

## The trade

A system that decides once has fewer ways out than you may be used to. There's no `variant` prop
to reach for when a design doesn't quite fit. What you get instead are refusals, each recorded
with a reason and an alternative, and every component's page lists its own. The one way out is
`style`, and a `style` value is easy to see in a review.

In return, the system stays consistent as your app grows, because its decisions are enforced
rather than left to memory.
