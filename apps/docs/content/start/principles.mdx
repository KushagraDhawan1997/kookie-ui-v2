Kookie prioritises correctness over ease of adoption. Some interface questions have measured
answers, and Kookie applies those answers for you. Other questions need design judgment, and
Kookie makes each of those decisions in one place, so your screens stay consistent as the app
grows.

## Measured limits

Decades of research inform interface design. Where a standard measurement exists, Kookie applies
it automatically, and no prop can override the result.

- **Fitts's law (1954):** The time it takes to move a pointer to a target increases as the
  target gets smaller or further away.
- **Tap targets:** A fingertip is about 10 millimetres across, which informs the 44-point
  minimum on Apple platforms and the 48-pixel minimum on Android. WCAG 2.2 sets a lower minimum
  of 24 pixels.
- **Gestalt principles (1923):** People group visual elements before they read them. Because
  distance carries meaning, the space between two elements changes how they are interpreted.
- **Contrast:** WCAG and APCA both measure whether text is legible against its background.

## How the limits shape components

These measurements set the default sizes throughout the system, and the sizes adapt to the
device without any work from you.

- **Touch screens:** A `Theme` detects whether people use a mouse or a finger. On a touch
  screen, controls grow to touch sizes, so a size 2 `Button` is 44 pixels tall.
- **Checkboxes:** A checkbox stays small so that it sits neatly beside one line of text. Its
  tappable area extends past the visible box, out to the size of a `Button` at the same size.
- **Type on phones:** You hold a phone closer to your eyes than a desktop screen, so small text
  sizes increase on touch devices. Large display sizes decrease on narrow windows, because fewer
  characters fit on a line.
- **Text fields:** On touch screens, a text field never uses a font smaller than 16 pixels. At
  smaller sizes, Safari on iOS zooms the page when the field receives focus.
- **Round controls:** With `radius="full"`, the ends of a control are fully round, so the curve
  starts close to the first and last letters. These controls get wider side padding, which keeps
  the label from looking cramped.

## Judgments

Where no measurement applies, a decision is a judgment. Corner radius, the accent hue, the
blur of a glass panel and the padding inside a card are all judgments.

Kookie keeps the two kinds apart. A measured limit is enforced, and you cannot override it. A
judgment is stored as one value, so a single edit changes it everywhere. Kookie never presents a
judgment as if it were a measurement, because a chosen number does not become more correct by
looking precise.

## Answer each question once

An inconsistent design system hides its cost at first. Your first and fifth screens can look
right. By the twentieth screen, the same question has been answered differently in a dozen
files, and the screens no longer agree with each other.

Kookie answers each design question once, and every screen uses that answer. This is why a
component accepts a `tone` and an `emphasis` instead of a colour, and why there's no `variant`
prop or margin prop.

```tsx
// Say what the button means. The theme picks the colour, in light mode and dark.
<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="loud">
  Delete project
</Button>
```

## Components are organised by what they do

Kookie classifies components by their job rather than their appearance. Two components can
look almost the same and still do different jobs. When that happens, Kookie keeps them separate,
and each one gets its own name and props.

`Tabs` and `SegmentedControl` show the difference. Both render a row of segments. `Tabs` moves
you between views and replaces the content below, whereas a `SegmentedControl` sets a value and
leaves the surrounding content in place.

The two controls also announce themselves differently to screen readers. `Tabs` reports a tab
list and names the panel that each tab shows. A `SegmentedControl` reports a radio group, where
the arrow keys change the value. Because the behaviour differs, no prop converts one into the
other.

To choose between two similar components, ask what each one does on its own. You can switch
views without setting a value, and you can set a value without switching views. The
[Vocabulary](/start/vocabulary) chapter lists other pairs that look alike.

## Components and patterns

A component is complete on its own: a `Button` includes its label, its icon and its padding. A
pattern is an arrangement of several components that your app puts together. An app frame is a
pattern, because the header, the sidebar, the content and the way they respond to the window
depend on your app. `Shell` arranges the panes, and you decide what goes in them. The
[Navigation](/patterns/navigation) chapter shows how to build a frame for each window size.

A child never changes its parent's settings. If you need a child to change its parent, such as a
sidebar that sets the page margin, you are building a pattern. Build it in your app rather than
looking for a component that provides it.

## The trade-off

A system that makes its decisions in one place gives you fewer overrides than you may be used
to. When a design does not fit, there's no `variant` prop to reach for, and TypeScript rejects
the props that Kookie does not accept. Each component page lists the props it does not accept,
with the reason and an alternative. `style` and `className` are still available, and both are
easy to spot in a code review.

In return, the system stays consistent as your app grows, because its decisions are enforced
rather than left to memory.
