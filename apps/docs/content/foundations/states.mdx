Every control reads one set of states: resting, hover, press, focus, disabled and the error
states. The system defines them once and no component changes them, so a component declares
which settings it has and never how a state looks.

The reason is arithmetic, because multiplying the components by the loudness levels and by the
states produces more combinations than a person can keep consistent by hand.

Two things that are not controls also respond to a pointer, and each behaves differently from
one.

**A `Link` is a run of words rather than a box.** It stays selectable and wraps across lines, so
it receives the focus ring and its own hover without a control box.

**A `Card` rendered as a button or a label** carries the shape of a control, so it receives every
control state and reads the same colour steps and the same ring.

## Hover and press are steps on one scale

Hover moves the fill one step and press moves it two, and no loudness level carries its own
amount of feedback.

A medium button rests on the third step, moves to the fourth on hover and the fifth on press. A
loud button rests on its solid colour, moves one step up on hover and one more on press. A quiet
button holds no fill at rest, so it joins the scale at hover, running transparent, then the third
step, then the fourth.

The colours differ at each level while the *amount* of change stays the same, which keeps two
controls at the same loudness in step with each other.

One step also reads as the same amount of change on every hue, because the lightness scale under
the colours is perceptually even. On an ordinary sRGB palette one step looks larger on blue than
on yellow, which makes the feedback vary from colour to colour.

## A label stays readable in every state

A label must meet its contrast target against every background its loudness level reaches,
because the resting background alone is not enough and a label that reads at rest can fail on
press.

## Hover only applies where a pointer can hover

Every hover rule sits inside `@media (hover: hover)`, because a touch device reports a false
hover on tap and keeps it until the next tap. Without the media query, a button you tapped would
hold its hover colour after your finger leaves.

Press carries no media query, because press is the only feedback a touch device gives you.

## One focus ring

One ring shows focus across the whole system. It is an outline with a small gap between it and
the box, and an outline follows the shape of the corner, pills included, without changing the
layout. A shadow does neither.

The ring reports one thing, the location of focus, and that information stays the same
everywhere, so the ring reads the accent colour whatever tone the control carries. A destructive
button receives an accent ring as well.

WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.4.13 set the minimum: at least two pixels wide, with a gap, at 3:1 contrast
against the colour beside it. The ring meets those limits in light mode and in dark mode through
a different colour in each, because a colour that reads on a white page disappears on a dark one.

The error state is the one exception, since an invalid field shows its ring in the error colour.
An error is not a choice in the way a tone is.

## A field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it

You do not press a field, you enter it, and the box must show that however you arrived. Every
native text input behaves this way, so a field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it rather
than only when you arrive from the keyboard.

The ring lands on the input that holds focus and never on the wrapper around it, while a button
in the field's trailing slot receives its own ring like any other control, which leaves exactly
one ring visible at a time.

## A state beats a decoration

The tick on a checkbox is a decoration, whereas invalid and disabled are states, and a state
wins over a decoration. The stylesheet enforces that order, so a ticked checkbox that is also
invalid shows the invalid border rather than its resting one.

Disabled wins over invalid, and the order is fixed.

## Disabled changes colours rather than fading

Disabled replaces the text, fill and border colours with the disabled palette, stops hover and
press, and returns the cursor to an ordinary arrow rather than `not-allowed`, which no native
platform uses.

Disabled never reaches for opacity, because opacity on a tinted surface adds the colour beneath
it and cancels every contrast guarantee the colour generator made. New colours keep the control
readable *and* show clearly that it is off.

## A pressable card behaves like a button

A `Card` rendered as a button or a link receives every control state, so its fill changes on
hover and on press and the ring appears exactly as it does on a button, while the card itself
holds its position.

**A disabled card shows exactly what a disabled button shows.** The fill lightens, the words dim,
the cursor returns to an ordinary arrow, and the shadow goes, because a shadow says you can pick
a thing up and you cannot pick up a disabled card. Nothing responds to the pointer after that.

## A selectable card is the label of a radio

There's no `selected` prop, because choosing one of several options is a radio group, which
makes a selectable card a card that **is** the label of a real radio.

```tsx
<RadioGroup defaultValue="pro" aria-label="Plan">
  <Grid columns="repeat(3, minmax(0, 1fr))" gap="5">
    <Card size="3" render={<label />}>
      <Stack gap="3">
        <Flex justify="space-between" align="center">
          <Text size="3" weight="medium">Pro</Text>
          <Radio value="pro" />
        </Flex>
        <Text size="5">$20</Text>
      </Stack>
    </Card>
    {/* the other cards, each with its own Radio */}
  </Grid>
</RadioGroup>
```

The whole card is clickable, because that is how a label behaves, and the keyboard behaviour,
the form value and the announcement all arrive from the radio. The system adds one thing, which
is that the chosen card's border changes colour. A `selected` prop would be a second way to say
what the radio already says, and nothing would keep the two values in agreement.

A checkbox behaves the same way, so a board where you choose several cards works identically.
Put the checkbox or radio wherever the layout needs it, and because chosen and focused read
different colours, a card can show both at once.

## Validity comes from your code, not a prop

There's no `invalid` prop, because nothing decides to *look* invalid. The system reads
`aria-invalid`, and it also reads Base UI's `data-invalid` attribute when a field sits inside a
`Field`.

```tsx
<Stack gap="4">
  <Flex render={<label />} direction="column" gap="2">
    <Text size="2" weight="medium">Work email</Text>
    <TextField
      type="email"
      value={email}
      onChange={(event) => setEmail(event.target.value)}
      aria-invalid={emailError ? true : undefined}
      aria-describedby={emailError ? "email-error" : undefined}
    />
    {emailError ? (
      <Text id="email-error" size="2" tone="destructive">{emailError}</Text>
    ) : null}
  </Flex>
  <Button emphasis="loud" disabled={!email}>Send invite</Button>
</Stack>
```

Your validation sets the state, the border and the ring respond to `aria-invalid`, and you write
the message as a sibling element.

The text a person typed is content, so it stays fully readable while the box carries the state
through its border and its ring. WCAG 1.4.1 rules out colour on its own, and the border is the
part the system supplies while the message is the part you supply. Add error text to any field
that shows none.

## The decision is recorded on the element

States are stylesheet rules that read data attributes, so the element records every decision,
where you can read them in the inspector and a lint rule can read them too.

```html
<button type="button" class="kui-control kui-button"
        data-size="2" data-tone="destructive" data-emphasis="quiet">Delete</button>
```

None of those values is a colour and no class contains one, because the element carries the
meaning and the loudness while the theme turns them into a colour.

## Use tone for meaning, emphasis for loudness

Two independent questions take two independent props, and there's no `variant`. `tone` says what
an action means, `emphasis` compares it with the actions near it as loud, medium or quiet, and
`bordered` works at all three levels.

A single combined appearance prop mixes the two questions, which is how systems arrive at names
like `secondary-outline-destructive`. Every combination then needs a new name, the names refuse
to combine, and the prop stops describing what the button does.

A `primary | secondary | danger` prop also says less than two props can, because it cannot say
"quiet and destructive", and a delete link is one of the most ordinary things an interface must
show.

```tsx
// One question at a time, and a quiet destructive action is expressible.
<Button emphasis="loud">Save changes</Button>
<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>
```

Use `bordered` as one more step in the loudness sequence rather than as a decoration, running
quiet, then quiet with a border, then medium. A border on a loud button changes nothing, because
a hairline has nothing to separate against a solid fill.

The prop is `emphasis` rather than `primary` or `secondary`, because those words mix the two
questions again. Keeping meaning and loudness separate also lets tools find two loud actions on
one screen.
