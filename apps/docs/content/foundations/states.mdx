Every control shares one set of states: resting, hover, press, focus, disabled, and the
error states. The system defines them once, and no component changes them. A component says
which settings it has, and it never says what a state should look like.

The reason is arithmetic: components times loudness levels times states is more combinations
than anyone can keep consistent by hand.

Two things that aren't controls still respond to a pointer, and they behave differently.

**A `Link` is a run of words**, not a box. It stays selectable and wraps across lines, so it
takes the focus ring and a hover of its own, and no control box.

**A `Card` rendered as a button or a label** is control-shaped, so it takes the whole thing:
the same colour steps, the same ring, the same timing. Only the press distances are its own.

## Hover and press are steps on one scale

Hover moves the fill one step. Press moves it two. No loudness level invents its own amount of
feedback.

A medium button rests on the third step, hovers to the fourth, and presses to the fifth. A
loud button rests on its solid colour, hovers one above, and presses one further. A quiet
button rests on nothing at all, so it joins the scale at hover: transparent, then the third
step, then the fourth.

The colours differ at each level. The *amount* of change doesn't, so two controls at the same
loudness match.

One step also looks like the same amount of change on every hue, because the lightness scale
underneath is perceptually even. On an ordinary sRGB palette, one step looks bigger on blue
than on yellow, and the feedback would differ from colour to colour.

## A label stays readable in every state

A label has to clear its contrast target against every background its loudness level uses,
not just the resting one. A label that looks fine at rest can fail on press.

## Hover only applies where a pointer can hover

Every hover rule sits inside `@media (hover: hover)`. A touch device fakes hover on tap and
holds it until the next tap, so without that guard a button you tapped keeps its hover colour
after your finger leaves.

Press has no such guard, because press is the only feedback a touch device gives you.

## One focus ring

One ring serves the whole system: an outline, held a little off the box. An outline follows
the corner shape natively, pills included, and it can't change the layout. A shadow does
neither.

The ring shows one thing: where focus is. The answer is the same everywhere, so the ring is
always the accent colour whatever tone the control carries. A destructive button gets an
accent ring.

WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.4.13 set the floor: at least two pixels, an offset, and 3:1 contrast against
what is beside it. The ring clears that in both light and dark, using a different colour in each,
because a colour that stands out against a white page disappears against a dark one.

The error state is the one exception: an invalid field rings in the error colour, because an
error isn't a choice the way a tone is.

## A field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it

You don't press a field. You enter it, and the box has to show that however you got there.
Every native text input works this way, so a field rings whenever the caret is in it, not only
when you arrive by keyboard.

The ring lands on the input that has focus, never on the wrapper around it. A button in the
field's trailing slot gets its own ring, like any other control, so you always see exactly one.

## A state beats a decoration

The tick on a checkbox is decoration. Invalid and disabled are states, and a state wins. The
stylesheet enforces that, so a ticked checkbox that is also invalid draws the invalid border
rather than its resting one.

Disabled beats invalid. The order is fixed.

## Disabled changes colours rather than fading

Disabled swaps the text, fills, and borders for the disabled palette. Hover and press stop
firing, and the cursor becomes an ordinary arrow. It never becomes `not-allowed`, which no
native platform uses.

It doesn't use opacity. Opacity stacks on a tinted surface and quietly voids every contrast
guarantee the generator made. Swapping the colours keeps the control readable *and* clearly
off.

## A pressable card behaves like a button

A `Card` rendered as a button or a link takes the whole state machine. Its fill steps on hover
and press, the ring lands the same way, and it moves on the same clocks.

Only the distances are its own: a card sinks one pixel and shrinks to 0.995 where a button
shrinks to 0.975. Scale is relative, so a button's factor on a 400-pixel card would move every
edge five pixels, and that looks like the page flexing.

**A disabled card shows exactly what a disabled button shows.** The fill recedes, the words
dim, the cursor becomes an ordinary arrow, and the shadow goes. A shadow says a thing can be
picked up, and a disabled card can't be. After that, nothing responds to the pointer.

## A selectable card is the label of a radio

There's no `selected` prop. Choosing one of several is a radio group, so a card you can
choose is a card that **is** the label of a real radio.

```tsx
<RadioGroup defaultValue="pro" aria-label="Plan">
  <Grid columns="repeat(3, minmax(0, 1fr))" gap="5">
    <Card size="3" render={<label />}>
      <Stack gap="3">
        <Flex justify="space-between" align="center">
          <Text size="3" weight="medium">Pro</Text>
          <Radio value="pro" />
        </Flex>
        <Text size="5">$20</Text>
      </Stack>
    </Card>
    {/* the other cards, each with its own Radio */}
  </Grid>
</RadioGroup>
```

The whole card is clickable, because that's what a label does. The keyboard behaviour, the
form value, and the announcement all come from the radio. The system adds one thing: the
chosen card's border changes colour. A `selected` prop would be a second way of saying what the radio already
says, and nothing would keep the two in agreement.

A checkbox works the same way, so a board where you can choose several behaves identically.
Put the mark wherever the layout needs it. Being chosen and being focused use different colours,
so a card can show both at once.

## Validity comes from your code, not a prop

There's no `invalid` prop, because nothing decides to *look* invalid. The system reads
`aria-invalid`, and Base UI's own `data-invalid` when a field sits inside a `Field`.

```tsx
<Stack gap="4">
  <Flex render={<label />} direction="column" gap="2">
    <Text size="2" weight="medium">Work email</Text>
    <TextField
      type="email"
      value={email}
      onChange={(event) => setEmail(event.target.value)}
      aria-invalid={emailError ? true : undefined}
      aria-describedby={emailError ? "email-error" : undefined}
    />
    {emailError ? (
      <Text id="email-error" size="2" tone="destructive">{emailError}</Text>
    ) : null}
  </Flex>
  <Button emphasis="loud" disabled={!email}>Send invite</Button>
</Stack>
```

The state comes from your validation. The border and ring respond to `aria-invalid`. The message
is a sibling you write.

The text someone typed is content, so it stays fully readable. The box carries the state in
its border and ring. WCAG 1.4.1 says colour on its own is not enough, and the border is only
the system's half of that. The message is your half. If a field shows no error text, add one.

## The decision is recorded on the element

Because states are stylesheet rules reading data attributes, the element records the
decisions. You can read them in the inspector, and so can a lint rule.

```html
<button type="button" class="kui-control kui-button"
        data-size="2" data-tone="destructive" data-emphasis="quiet">Delete</button>
```

None of those values is a colour, and no class encodes one. The meaning and the loudness sit
on the element, and the theme answers with a colour.

## Use tone for meaning, emphasis for loudness

Two independent questions get two independent props, and there's no `variant`. `tone` says
what an action means. `emphasis` ranks it against its neighbours: loud, medium, quiet. `bordered`
works at all three.

One combined appearance prop fuses those questions, which is how systems end up with names like
`secondary-outline-destructive`. Every combination needs a new name, the names stop composing,
and eventually the prop stops describing what the button does.

A `primary | secondary | danger` prop is also less expressive than two props. It can't say
"quiet and destructive", and a delete link is one of the most ordinary things an interface has
to express.

```tsx
// One question at a time, and a quiet destructive action is expressible.
<Button emphasis="loud">Save changes</Button>
<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>
```

Treat `bordered` as one more step in the loudness sequence rather than as decoration: quiet,
then quiet with a border, then medium. Loud with a border is allowed and changes nothing,
because a hairline against a solid fill has nothing to separate.

The prop is called `emphasis` rather than `primary` or `secondary`, because those words
bring the fused question back. Keeping meaning and loudness apart also lets tooling notice two
loud actions on one screen.
