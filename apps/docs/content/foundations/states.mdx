Every control uses one set of states: resting, hover, press, focus, disabled and the error
states. The system defines these states one time, and no component changes them. A component
says which settings it has. It never says how a state looks.

The reason is arithmetic. Multiply the components by the loudness levels and by the states.
The result is more combinations than a person can keep consistent by hand.

Two things that are not controls also respond to a pointer. They behave differently from a
control.

**A `Link` is a run of words**, not a box. It stays selectable and it wraps across lines. So it
gets the focus ring and its own hover, but no control box.

**A `Card` rendered as a button or a label** has the shape of a control, so it gets all of the
control states. It uses the same colour steps, the same ring and the same timing. Only its
press distances are different.

## Hover and press are steps on one scale

Hover moves the fill one step. Press moves the fill two steps. No loudness level uses its own
amount of feedback.

A medium button rests on the third step. It moves to the fourth step on hover and to the fifth
step on press. A loud button rests on its solid colour. It moves one step up on hover and one
more step on press. A quiet button has no fill at rest, so it starts the scale at hover. The
sequence is transparent, then the third step, then the fourth step.

The colours are different at each level. The *amount* of change is the same. So two controls
at the same loudness match.

One step also looks like the same amount of change on every hue. The lightness scale under the
colours is perceptually even. On an ordinary sRGB palette, one step looks bigger on blue than
on yellow. The feedback then changes from colour to colour.

## A label stays readable in every state

A label must meet its contrast target against every background that its loudness level uses.
The resting background is not sufficient. A label that is readable at rest can fail on press.

## Hover only applies where a pointer can hover

Every hover rule is inside `@media (hover: hover)`. A touch device shows a false hover on tap.
It keeps that hover until the next tap. Without the media query, a button that you tapped keeps
its hover colour after your finger leaves.

Press has no media query around it. Press is the only feedback that a touch device gives you.

## One focus ring

One ring shows focus in the whole system. The ring is an outline, with a small gap between it
and the box. An outline follows the shape of the corner, and this includes pills. An outline
cannot change the layout. A shadow can do neither of these things.

The ring shows one thing: the location of focus. That information is the same everywhere. So
the ring always uses the accent colour, whatever tone the control has. A destructive button
also gets an accent ring.

WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.4.13 set the minimum. The ring must be at least two pixels wide, with a gap.
It must have 3:1 contrast against the colour next to it. The ring meets these limits in light
mode and in dark mode. It uses a different colour in each mode. A colour that is clear on a
white page disappears on a dark page.

The error state is the one exception. An invalid field shows its ring in the error colour. An
error is not a choice in the way that a tone is a choice.

## A field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it

You do not press a field. You enter it, and the box must show this, however you entered it.
Every native text input works like this. So a field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it,
not only when you arrive with the keyboard.

The ring goes on the input that has focus. It never goes on the wrapper around the input. A
button in the trailing slot of the field gets its own ring, as any other control does. So you
always see exactly one ring.

## A state beats a decoration

The tick on a checkbox is a decoration. Invalid and disabled are states, and a state wins over
a decoration. The stylesheet enforces this order. If a ticked checkbox is also invalid, it
shows the invalid border and not its resting border.

Disabled wins over invalid. This order is fixed.

## Disabled changes colours rather than fading

Disabled replaces the text, fill and border colours with the disabled palette. Hover and press
stop. The cursor becomes an ordinary arrow. It never becomes `not-allowed`, because no native
platform uses that cursor.

Disabled does not use opacity. On a tinted surface, opacity adds to the colour below it. This
cancels every contrast guarantee from the colour generator. New colours keep the control
readable, *and* they show clearly that it is off.

## A pressable card behaves like a button

A `Card` rendered as a button or a link gets all of the control states. Its fill moves on hover
and on press. The ring appears in the same way. The card moves on the same timing as a button.

Only the distances are different. A card moves down one pixel and shrinks to 0.995. A button
shrinks to 0.975. Scale is relative. On a 400-pixel card, the button factor moves every edge
five pixels, and the page then seems to bend.

**A disabled card shows exactly what a disabled button shows.** The fill becomes lighter, the
words become dim, the cursor becomes an ordinary arrow and the shadow goes. A shadow tells you
that you can pick a thing up, and you cannot pick up a disabled card. After that, nothing
responds to the pointer.

## A selectable card is the label of a radio

There's no `selected` prop. When you choose one of several options, that is a radio group. So a
card that you can choose is a card that **is** the label of a real radio.

```tsx
<RadioGroup defaultValue="pro" aria-label="Plan">
  <Grid columns="repeat(3, minmax(0, 1fr))" gap="5">
    <Card size="3" render={<label />}>
      <Stack gap="3">
        <Flex justify="space-between" align="center">
          <Text size="3" weight="medium">Pro</Text>
          <Radio value="pro" />
        </Flex>
        <Text size="5">$20</Text>
      </Stack>
    </Card>
    {/* the other cards, each with its own Radio */}
  </Grid>
</RadioGroup>
```

You can click the whole card, because a label works that way. The keyboard behaviour, the form
value and the announcement all come from the radio. The system adds one thing: the border of
the chosen card changes colour. A `selected` prop would be a second way to say what the radio
already says. Nothing would keep the two values the same.

A checkbox works in the same way. So a board where you can choose several cards behaves in the
same way. Put the checkbox or radio wherever the layout needs it. Chosen and focused use
different colours, so a card can show both at the same time.

## Validity comes from your code, not a prop

There's no `invalid` prop, because nothing decides to *look* invalid. The system reads
`aria-invalid`. When a field is inside a `Field`, the system also reads the `data-invalid`
attribute from Base UI.

```tsx
<Stack gap="4">
  <Flex render={<label />} direction="column" gap="2">
    <Text size="2" weight="medium">Work email</Text>
    <TextField
      type="email"
      value={email}
      onChange={(event) => setEmail(event.target.value)}
      aria-invalid={emailError ? true : undefined}
      aria-describedby={emailError ? "email-error" : undefined}
    />
    {emailError ? (
      <Text id="email-error" size="2" tone="destructive">{emailError}</Text>
    ) : null}
  </Flex>
  <Button emphasis="loud" disabled={!email}>Send invite</Button>
</Stack>
```

Your validation sets the state. The border and the ring respond to `aria-invalid`. You write
the message as a sibling element.

The text that a person typed is content, so it stays fully readable. The box shows the state
with its border and its ring. WCAG 1.4.1 says that colour alone is not sufficient. The border
is the part that the system supplies. The message is the part that you supply. If a field shows
no error text, add error text.

## The decision is recorded on the element

States are stylesheet rules that read data attributes. So the element records the decisions.
You can read them in the inspector, and a lint rule can read them too.

```html
<button type="button" class="kui-control kui-button"
        data-size="2" data-tone="destructive" data-emphasis="quiet">Delete</button>
```

None of these values is a colour, and no class contains a colour. The meaning and the loudness
are on the element. The theme turns them into a colour.

## Use tone for meaning, emphasis for loudness

Two independent questions get two independent props, and there's no `variant`. `tone` says
what an action means. `emphasis` compares it with the actions near it: loud, medium or quiet.
`bordered` works at all three levels.

One combined appearance prop mixes the two questions. That is how systems get names like
`secondary-outline-destructive`. Each combination needs a new name, and the names do not
combine. Finally, the prop no longer describes what the button does.

A `primary | secondary | danger` prop can also say less than two props. It cannot say "quiet
and destructive". A delete link is one of the most ordinary things an interface must show.

```tsx
// One question at a time, and a quiet destructive action is expressible.
<Button emphasis="loud">Save changes</Button>
<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>
```

Use `bordered` as one more step in the loudness sequence, not as a decoration. The sequence is
quiet, then quiet with a border, then medium. You can add a border to a loud button, but it
changes nothing. A hairline has nothing to separate against a solid fill.

The prop is `emphasis`, not `primary` or `secondary`, because those words mix the two
questions again. When meaning and loudness stay separate, tools can also find two loud actions
on one screen.
