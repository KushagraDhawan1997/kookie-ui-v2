Every control in this system shares one set of states: resting, hover, press, focus, disabled,
and the error states. The shared layer defines them once, and no component changes them. A
component says which settings it has; it never says what a state should look like.

The reason is arithmetic. Components, times loudness levels, times states is far more
combinations than anyone can keep consistent by hand.

Two things that are not controls still respond to a pointer, and they take different parts of
this chapter.

**A `Link` is a run of words**, not a box. It stays selectable and wraps across lines, so it
takes the focus ring and a hover of its own and none of the geometry.

**A `Card` rendered as a button or a label** is control-shaped, so it takes the whole thing:
the same colour steps, the same ring, the same timing. Only the press distances are its own.

## Hover and press are steps on one scale

Hover moves the fill one step. Press moves it two. No loudness level invents its own amount of
feedback.

A medium button rests on the third step, hovers to the fourth and presses to the fifth. A loud
button rests on its solid colour, hovers one above and presses one further. A quiet button rests
on nothing at all, which is not a step, so it joins the scale at hover: transparent, then the
third step, then the fourth.

The colours differ at each level; the *amount* of change does not. Two controls at the same
loudness match because they are reading the same values.

One step also looks like the same amount of change on every hue, because the lightness scale
underneath is perceptually even. On an ordinary sRGB palette, one step looks bigger on blue than
on yellow, and your interaction feedback would quietly differ per colour.

## A label stays readable in every state

A label has to clear its contrast target against every background its loudness level uses, not
just the resting one. Press is where a label that looked fine at rest fails.

## Hover only applies where a pointer can hover

Every hover rule sits inside `@media (hover: hover)`. A touch device fakes hover on tap and
holds it until the next tap, so without that guard a button you tapped keeps its hover colour
after your finger leaves.

Press has no such guard, because press is the only feedback a touch device gives you.

## There is one focus ring

One ring serves the whole system: an outline, held a little off the box. An outline follows the
corner shape natively — pills included — and it cannot change the layout. A shadow does neither.

The ring answers one question: where is focus? That question is the same everywhere, so the ring
is always the accent colour whatever tone the control carries. A destructive button gets an
accent ring.

WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.4.13 set the floor: at least two pixels, an offset, and 3:1 contrast against
what is beside it. The ring clears that in both light and dark, using a different colour in each,
because a colour that stands out against a white page disappears against a dark one.

The error state is the one exception: an invalid field rings in the error colour. A tone is
something you choose. A state is not.

## A field shows its ring whenever the caret is in it

You do not press a field, you enter it — and the box has to show that however you got there.
Every native text input works this way, so a field rings whenever the caret is in it, not only
when you arrive by keyboard.

The ring lands on the input that has focus, never on the wrapper around it. A button in the
field's trailing slot gets its own ring, like any other control, so you always see exactly one.

## A state beats a decoration

The tick on a checkbox is decoration. Invalid and disabled are states, and a state wins. The
stylesheet enforces that through specificity rather than the order rules happen to be written
in, so a ticked checkbox that is also invalid draws the invalid border rather than its resting
one.

Disabled beats invalid. The order is fixed.

## Disabled changes colours; it never fades

Disabled swaps the text, fills and borders for the disabled palette. Hover and press stop
firing, and the cursor becomes an ordinary arrow — never `not-allowed`, which no native platform
uses.

It does not use opacity. Opacity stacks on a tinted surface and quietly voids every contrast
guarantee the generator made. Swapping the colours keeps the control readable *and* clearly off.

## A pressable card behaves like a button

A `Card` rendered as a button or a link takes the whole state machine. Its fill steps on hover
and press, the ring lands the same way, and it moves on the same clocks.

Only the distances are its own: a card sinks one pixel and shrinks to 0.995 where a button
shrinks to 0.975. Scale is relative, so a button's factor on a 400-pixel card would move every
edge five pixels, and that reads as the page flexing.

**A disabled card shows exactly what a disabled button shows.** The fill recedes, the words dim,
the cursor stops promising anything, and the shadow goes. Losing the shadow is a statement
rather than a dimming: a shadow says a thing can be picked up, and a dead card cannot be. After
that nothing responds to the pointer.

## A selectable card is the label of a radio

There is no `selected` prop. Choosing one of several is a radio group, so a card you can choose
is a card that **is** the label of a real radio.

```tsx
<RadioGroup defaultValue="pro" aria-label="Plan">
  <Grid columns="repeat(3, minmax(0, 1fr))" gap="5">
    <Card size="3" render={<label />}>
      <Stack gap="3">
        <Flex justify="space-between" align="center">
          <Text size="3" weight="medium">Pro</Text>
          <Radio value="pro" />
        </Flex>
        <Text size="5">$20</Text>
      </Stack>
    </Card>
    {/* the other cards, each with its own Radio */}
  </Grid>
</RadioGroup>
```

The whole card is clickable, because that is what a label does. The keyboard behaviour, the form
value and the announcement all come from the radio. The system adds one thing: the chosen card's
border changes colour. A `selected` prop would be a second way of saying what the radio already
says, and nothing would keep the two in agreement.

The same works with a checkbox, so a board where you can choose several lights up the same way.
Put the mark wherever the layout wants it. Being chosen and being focused use different colours,
so a card can show both at once.

## Validity comes from your code, not a prop

There is no `invalid` prop, because nothing decides to *look* invalid. The system reads
`aria-invalid`, and Base UI's own `data-invalid` when a field sits inside a `Field`.

```tsx
<Stack gap="4">
  <Flex render={<label />} direction="column" gap="2">
    <Text size="2" weight="medium">Work email</Text>
    <TextField
      type="email"
      value={email}
      onChange={(event) => setEmail(event.target.value)}
      aria-invalid={emailError ? true : undefined}
      aria-describedby={emailError ? "email-error" : undefined}
    />
    {emailError ? (
      <Text id="email-error" size="2" tone="destructive">{emailError}</Text>
    ) : null}
  </Flex>
  <Button emphasis="loud" disabled={!email}>Send invite</Button>
</Stack>
```

The state comes from your validation. The border and ring respond to `aria-invalid`. The message
is a sibling you write.

The text someone typed is content, so it stays fully readable; the box carries the state in its
border and ring. WCAG 1.4.1 says colour on its own is not enough, and the border is only the
system's half of that. The message is your half. A field that renders no error text is a
composition problem rather than a missing feature.

## The decision is recorded on the element

Because states are stylesheet rules reading data attributes, the element itself records what was
decided. You can read the design off it in the inspector, and so can a lint rule.

```html
<button type="button" class="kui-control kui-button"
        data-size="2" data-tone="destructive" data-emphasis="quiet">Delete</button>
```

Nothing there is a resolved colour, and no class encodes one. The meaning and the loudness are
on the element, and the theme answers with a colour.

## Use tone for meaning, emphasis for loudness

Two independent questions get two independent props, and there is no `variant`. `tone` says what
an action means. `emphasis` ranks it against its neighbours: loud, medium, quiet. `bordered`
works at all three.

One combined appearance prop fuses those questions, which is how systems end up with names like
`secondary-outline-destructive`. Every combination needs a new name, the names stop composing,
and eventually the prop stops describing what the button does.

A `primary | secondary | danger` prop is also less expressive than two props. It cannot say
"quiet and destructive", and a delete link is one of the most ordinary things an interface has
to express.

```tsx
// One question at a time, and a quiet destructive action is expressible.
<Button emphasis="loud">Save changes</Button>
<Button tone="destructive" emphasis="quiet">Delete</Button>
```

Treat `bordered` as one more step in the loudness sequence rather than as decoration: quiet,
then quiet with a border, then medium. Loud with a border is legal and does nothing, because a hairline against a
solid fill contains nothing.

Call the setting emphasis rather than "primary" or "secondary". Those words bring the fused
question back — and keeping them apart is what lets tooling notice two loud actions on one
screen. A prop that only describes an appearance never learns what the button is for.
