Material is what the application is built of: one value on the Theme, four rungs, and no
component picks one. Its job is backdrop defense — keeping a foreground legible over whatever
passes behind it — which is also why it expresses only where something actually passes behind.

## One material for the whole app

```tsx
<Theme material="regular">
  <App />
</Theme>
```

The rungs are `solid`, `thin`, `regular` and `thick`. `solid` is the default, and it is a
material rather than the absence of one: the rung where light stops passing through. That is
also why this is not a boolean — `glass` would still owe a thickness beside it, which is two
props for one fact.

No component takes a material. The per-subtree escape is a nested `<Theme material>`, the same
escape every other axis has.

The shape tried first was a per-family ladder: the theme picks a rung, a dialog sits one above
it, a control one below. One question killed it — what happens when the theme says `thick`?
**There is no rung above `thick`.** Clamping at the top is an exception, and the same clamp at
the bottom makes `solid` stop meaning solid.

There was never a ladder to walk. A table and a chair made of the same oak are the same oak;
they do not get oak plus one. What makes a dialog read heavier than a menu is coverage and its
scrim, not a second thickness: the same glass over 900 pixels of application obscures far more
than over a 170-pixel menu.

## Glass expresses where there is a backdrop

One value for the scope, but *expression* is gated on placement. Glass over calm ground blurs
nothing and still pays a full backdrop readback, so the theme's material reaches only components
genuinely over something.

Mark the **place**, not each control:

```tsx
<Box backdrop>
  <Flex gap="2">
    <Button>Crop</Button>
    <Button>Rotate</Button>
    <TextField placeholder="Layer name" />
  </Flex>
</Box>
```

`backdrop` is a boolean placement fact, never a thickness — a toolbar of five controls is one
fact, not five props. `Card`, `Button`, `TextField`, `TextArea`, `SelectTrigger` and
`SegmentedControl` each take it as the one-off escape, and `backdrop={false}` re-marks a
sub-region as calm. Resolution order is the component's own prop, then the region, then solid.
Floating panes pass it by construction, because a popup covers the application by definition.

An unmarked in-flow control resolves `solid` and pays nothing. That is the performance half and
the reason the rule exists: before it, `thin` on a Theme made every control on the page carry a
backdrop filter to defend against a backdrop that was not there.

## Glass does not stack

A pane that paints a veil scopes its subtree, and every member below resolves to the veil's
alpha with no filter and no lens. You cannot compose glass on glass, and you are not asked not
to — the composition is unreachable rather than discouraged.

The reason is physical rather than stylistic. A pane reads as glass because it defocuses what is
behind it; on top of a second pane there is nothing left to defocus, so the child spends a full
readback blurring an already-blurred image and reads as a sticker.

A solid pane resets the region too, so an unmarked control on an opaque card stays solid. But a
solid surface **hosts** glass: an explicit statement made inside it wins, because a map or a
feed composed inside a card really does have something passing behind it.

```tsx
<Card>
  <Box backdrop>
    {/* the consumer's own map canvas paints here */}
    <Button>Recenter</Button>
  </Box>
</Card>
```

A `<Theme>` resets the pane scope as well, which is what keeps portals honest: a menu opened
from inside a glass card is glass again, because it paints over the page rather than inside the
card. Your own pane is a first-class case, and it is one line:

```tsx
const material = useMaterial({ backdrop: true });
return <div className="kui-surface" data-material={material} />;
```

## The veil is your own fill

Material is a fill **modifier**, never a fill: it takes whatever fill the component would have
painted, mixes it toward transparent at the thickness's alpha, and adds the filter. It never
chooses a colour of its own.

So both axes survive the glass. A loud accent button under glass is translucent accent; a medium
one is a soft veil; a quiet one is bare blur — the absence of a fill, frosted. The rung keeps its
own label pairing, because the fill is still the rung's, merely translucent.

The first cut did the opposite, with a white veil mixed over the page colour, and it erased tone
and loudness the moment anything under glass had either. If the veil can tint from neutral, it
can tint from whatever fill the rung already chose.

## When to reach for it, and when not

Reach for glass where something genuinely passes behind: a toolbar over a canvas, a panel over a
hero image, chrome that content scrolls *under*. Fixed chrome is the best case, because a
blurred element that itself moves re-samples its backdrop every frame.

Do not reach for it over a solid surface. Over calm ground it blurs nothing, reads as a muddy
smudge, and charges a readback per element for the privilege. That is not a policy, it is what
the material does.

There is no free glass either. A `layered` prop was proposed as exactly that — look like glass,
cost nothing — and the sealed composite it produced was measured and was not identical. Opting
out of the cost is the same act as opting out of the look, spelled `material="solid"` on a
nested Theme.

Two settings reach the glass on their own. `prefers-reduced-transparency` re-seals every pane
and drops the filter. `contrast="high"` leans on the glass rather than unmaking it: each
thickness takes a designed, more opaque alpha that raises a floor, so the thinnest rung travels
furthest and three rungs stay three. The rim stays — it is a gradient inside the pane, so it
cannot lower anyone's contrast, and removing it would only delete the cue that the pane is a
physical thing catching light.
