Material is what your app is made of. It's one setting on the Theme with four values, and no
component picks its own.

A material keeps a foreground readable over whatever passes behind it, so it only takes effect
where something passes behind a panel.

## One material for the whole app

```tsx
<Theme material="regular">
  <App />
</Theme>
```

The four values are `solid`, `thin`, `regular`, and `thick`, and `solid` is what you get by
default. Solid is the value where light stops passing through.

One value covers everything, because every panel in one app is made of the same material. A
dialog still feels heavier than a menu, because it covers far more of the screen and dims
everything behind it. The same glass over 900 pixels of app hides more than the same glass
over a 170-pixel menu.

No component takes a `material` prop. To change it for one part of your app, nest a
`<Theme material>`. That's the same escape every other setting uses.

## Glass needs something behind it

The Theme sets the value. Where you place things decides where it takes effect.

Glass over a plain background blurs nothing at all, but it still reads the background on every
repaint. So the material only reaches components you have marked as sitting over something.

Mark the place, not each control.

```tsx
<Theme material="regular">
  <Box className="editor-canvas">
    <Box backdrop p="2">
      <Flex gap="2" align="center">
        <Button emphasis="quiet">Crop</Button>
        <Button emphasis="quiet">Rotate</Button>
        <Separator orientation="vertical" />
        <TextField size="2" placeholder="Layer name" />
        <Button emphasis="loud">Export</Button>
      </Flex>
    </Box>
  </Box>
</Theme>
```

One `backdrop` marks the toolbar. Every control inside it becomes glass, keeps its own
loudness, and shows the artwork underneath.

`backdrop` is a boolean. It says where a panel sits. It never says how thick the glass is.

`Card`, `Button`, `TextField`, `TextArea`, `SelectTrigger`, `SegmentedControl`, `Notice`,
`Chip`, and `Composer` each take `backdrop` when you want a single control to be glass.
`backdrop={false}` marks a smaller region as plain again.

The five `Shell` panels take it too: `ShellHeader`, `ShellRail`, `ShellSidebar`,
`ShellInspector`, and `ShellBottom`. A sidebar that floats over a canvas is the clearest case
for glass in a whole app. `ShellContent` doesn't take it: the work area is the bottom of the
stack, so nothing ever passes behind it. A vibrant region inside the work area is still yours
to build. Put a `Box backdrop` or a `Card backdrop` in it, because a solid panel can hold
glass.

A panel doesn't need the prop once it becomes a drawer. On a narrow window, a nav column
slides over the page with a scrim under it, the same as a dialog, so it takes the theme's
material on its own. The prop matters while the panel stands in the frame.

The material resolves in this order: a panel that covers something, then the component's own
prop, then the region it sits in, then `solid`. A panel that covers the page marks itself,
because something is always behind it.

An unmarked control sitting in ordinary page flow stays solid and costs you nothing. That's
the performance reason for the rule: a blur filter on something with nothing behind it
re-reads the page on every paint and produces no visible result.

## Glass doesn't stack

A panel that paints translucent glass makes everything inside it solid. Those children take
the same transparency and add no blur of their own. You can't put glass on glass. The system
stops you, so it's not a rule you have to remember.

The reason is physical. A panel looks like glass because it blurs what's behind it. On top
of another panel, nothing is left to blur, so the child reads the background again, blurs an
already-blurred image, and ends up looking like a sticker.

A solid panel also resets the region, so an unmarked control on an opaque card stays solid.

A solid card can still hold glass. If you ask for it explicitly inside a card, you get it,
because a map or a feed inside a card really does have content passing behind it.

```tsx
<Card>
  <Box backdrop>
    {/* your own map canvas paints here */}
    <Button>Recenter</Button>
  </Box>
</Card>
```

A `<Theme>` resets it too, and that's why menus stay correct. A menu opened from a glass
card is glass again, because it paints over the page rather than inside the card.

Building your own panel takes one line:

```tsx
const material = useMaterial({ backdrop: true });
return <div className="kui-surface" data-material={material} />;
```

## Glass uses the component's own colour

A material changes a fill, and it isn't a fill itself. It takes the colour the component
would have painted, mixes it toward transparent, and adds the blur. It never picks a colour of
its own.

Your other two choices survive. A loud accent button under glass is translucent accent. A medium button is a soft tint. A quiet button is blur with no fill at all. Each one
keeps its own label colour, because the fill is still its own fill.

## When to use glass

Use glass where content genuinely passes behind a panel: a toolbar over a canvas, a panel over a
hero image, chrome that content scrolls underneath. Fixed chrome is the best case, because a
blurred element that moves has to re-read its background on every frame.

Don't use it over a solid surface. It blurs nothing, it looks like a smudge, and it costs a
background read per element.

Every glass panel costs a background read. To avoid that cost in one region, set
`material="solid"` on a nested Theme.

Two accessibility settings change the glass without you doing anything. If someone has asked for
reduced transparency, every panel becomes solid and the filter is removed. If someone has asked
for higher contrast, each value becomes more opaque. The thinnest moves furthest, so the
three values stay clearly different from each other.

The highlight along the top edge stays in both cases. It's a gradient inside the panel, so it
can't lower anyone's contrast, and it shows that the panel is a physical thing catching
light.
