One scale of nine steps sets the size of all text, including body copy, headings, button
labels, field values, and code. A step names a position on that scale rather than a font size,
and because the heaviest weight is semibold, you rank text by size and colour.

## Text and Heading share one scale

`Text` and `Heading` read the same nine steps through two different fonts. Selecting a step sets
the font size, the line height, and the letter spacing together, and no single ratio governs the
line height, because one ratio cannot hold at both ends of a scale running from 12 to 56 pixels.

A size carries the same meaning on both components, so `<Text size="6">` and `<Heading size="6">`
name the same step and differ only in font and default weight. Button labels read the same
scale, which keeps a button and the text beside it on the same steps.

## Size sets the step, render sets the element

The size of the text and its position in the document outline are two different questions, so
each one takes its own prop.

```tsx
// The page title: an h1 in the outline, at the top of the scale
<Heading size="8" render={<h1 />}>Billing</Heading>

// A card title: an h3 in the outline, two steps down
<Heading size="6" render={<h3 />}>Payment method</Heading>
```

`Heading` renders an `<h2>` at size 6 by default, because a page seldom needs a second `<h1>`,
while `Text` renders a `<span>` at size 3. Write `render={<p />}` when you want a paragraph.

No `level` or `as` prop does both jobs, because a single prop would force you to choose between
the correct outline for a screen reader and the correct size for the design.

## The heaviest weight is semibold

The three weights are `--font-weight-regular`, `--font-weight-medium`, and
`--font-weight-semibold`, and the `weight` prop accepts those names rather than numbers.

There is no bold, because the system already ranks importance two ways, through the nine steps
and the three text colours. A bold face is a third way that belongs to neither scale, and in
practice people apply it to everything.

A bold weight does not compile, since the type refuses it and the token does not exist. Set it
with `style` if your app genuinely needs a bold face.

Rank with size, and make text quieter with colour:

```tsx
// Not this: a heavier face at the same step
<Heading size="6" weight="semibold">Notifications</Heading>
<Text size="6" weight="medium">Choose what reaches you.</Text>

// This: a real step down, and a quieter colour
<Heading size="6">Notifications</Heading>
<Text size="3" emphasis="medium">Choose what reaches you.</Text>
```

## Three fonts

Your Theme sets `--font-heading`, `--font-body`, and `--font-mono`. Components read one of those
three and never a font stack, which makes the Theme the only place where you write one.

`Heading` reads the heading font, while `Text`, `Blockquote`, and `Link` read the body font.
`Code` reads the mono font at a slightly smaller size, because a monospaced face is wider and
taller and therefore looks one step larger at the same size.

`Kbd` is the exception, because a keycap reads the **body** font. A monospaced ⌘ narrows to fit a
fixed character width, and every platform shows shortcuts in its interface font.

## Emphasis picks a text colour

`emphasis` produces a different effect on each category of component:

- On a button, it selects a fill.
- On a card, it selects the styling.
- On text, it selects a colour.

On text, loud resolves to `--color-text`, medium to `--color-text-muted`, and quiet to
`--color-text-faint`, and adding a `tone` moves the same three levels onto that colour family.

Text rests at loud on purpose, because full contrast is the correct default for reading and a
grey paragraph reads as optional.

Quiet sits below the minimum contrast for body text, also on purpose. Use it for something
nobody must read or act on, such as a disabled option, and never for a full paragraph.

## How text responds to the device

Two things change with the device, and because they answer unrelated questions, the system
controls them separately.

- **Reading sizes increase on a touch screen.** Steps 1 to 4 rise, which takes body copy from 16
  to 18 pixels. The reason is distance, because you hold a phone near your face, and Apple
  publishes one Dynamic Type table for both iPhone and iPad, so the rule treats a tablet exactly
  like a phone.
- **Display sizes decrease on a narrow window.** Steps 8 and 9 fall, which takes a page title
  from 56 to 40 pixels. The reason is line length, because a 56-pixel title on a 375-pixel
  screen fits approximately seven characters per line, and a narrow browser window on a laptop
  hits the same limit.

Each rule moves only the steps it must, so the two never conflict on a phone, where both apply.
A tablet in landscape receives larger reading text and keeps its full-size titles, while a narrow
desktop window receives smaller titles and leaves the reading text alone.

Neither rule needs turning on. A size number names a role rather than a pixel value, so avoid
building a layout on a measurement you took from a step.

## The five steps to compose with

These five steps are the house style, and they are a judgment rather than a measurement. Every
distance on a screen follows a theme setting, whereas type has none, which is why the rule is
written down here instead.

**Use these five.** Page title **8**, section **7**, card or block title **6**, body **3**, and
label or small print **2**. A card title over body puts 24 pixels over 16 pixels, which is a
real step down. Steps 4, 5, and 9 cover the cases these five do not, such as a hero or a large
number, and belong only to those cases.

**Rank by size rather than by weight.** A 16-pixel title over a 14-pixel body is 1.14 times the
size, which is too small a difference to read as a hierarchy. Larger steps also carry tighter
letter spacing, which stays neutral from step 1 to step 3 and tightens from step 4 up, so a
small title never receives the tighter spacing.

**Do not use size 1 on a real screen.** Twelve pixels belongs only to text at the edge of a
screen, such as a footer, a legal line, or the controls of a dense table. Use the muted or faint
colour at 14 or 16 pixels when something matters less, and treat a screen that needs 12-pixel
text to fit as a spacing problem rather than a type problem.

This example shows the five steps together:

```tsx
<Stack gap="6">
  <Heading size="8" render={<h1 />}>Billing</Heading>

  <Stack gap="5">
    <Heading size="7" render={<h2 />}>Payment method</Heading>

    <Card size="3">
      <Stack gap="3">
        <Heading size="6" render={<h3 />}>Visa ending 4021</Heading>
        <Text size="3" render={<p />}>Renews on 3 September for 24 dollars.</Text>
        <Text size="2" emphasis="medium">Updated 3 August</Text>
      </Stack>
    </Card>
  </Stack>
</Stack>
```

The page title, the section, the card title, the body, and the small print occupy five different
steps, and the document outline stays correct because `render` selected the element while `size`
selected the step.
