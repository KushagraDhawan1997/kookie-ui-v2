Motion here covers two things: a colour changing, and something moving. Two separate clocks
drive them. No component sets a duration, and CSS handles every transition, so nothing runs in
JavaScript while someone is pressing a control.

## Two clocks

A colour or opacity change eases, and it is short. It uses `--motion-duration` and
`--motion-easing`.

Something that moves follows a spring. The configuration states the physics, and the generator
samples that model and bakes the result into a CSS `linear()` curve. The finished curve costs
the browser exactly what a `cubic-bezier` costs.

Keeping the two apart matters. Put both on one clock and the colour has to wait for the
movement, which makes the control feel late.

```css
/* Wrong: one clock for everything. */
.thing {
  transition: all 200ms ease;
}

/* Right: the colour eases, the movement springs. */
.thing {
  transition:
    background-color var(--motion-duration) var(--motion-easing),
    translate var(--motion-press) var(--motion-spring-stiff);
}
```

## A press changes colour immediately

A tap lasts about 60 milliseconds. An eased fill never reaches its colour in that time, so
someone tapping on a phone gets no colour response at all. That was measured on real hardware;
emulating touch on a desktop does not show it.

So a press sets its colour clock to zero. The fill lands on the very first frame, and the
movement springs underneath it. That is a floor: any motion added later still has to keep the
press immediate.

Hover is deliberately lopsided. It arrives in 80 milliseconds and leaves in 220. That is a
judgment: arriving is something the person did, and leaving is just the end of it.

## Each kind of component moves its own distance

A button sinks when pressed. It moves down *and* gets slightly smaller. Moving alone looks like
a slide; shrinking alone looks like it is backing away. Doing both reads as a press.

A pressable card sinks by its own smaller distances. Scale is relative, so a big card shrinking
by a button's factor would move each edge several pixels, and that reads as the page flexing.

A checkbox squashes instead, because it has no depth to sink into. A text field does not move at
all: your eye is resting inside it, and moving the box you are reading is unpleasant.

None of them sets a duration. The shared layer owns the clock and each component owns only the
distance, which is why two controls of the same size move alike.

That is the entire motion API. Everything below already moves correctly, and not one line sets a
duration, a distance or an easing.

```tsx
<Card size="3">
  <Stack gap="4">
    <Heading size="6">Deploy to production</Heading>

    <Flex render={<label />} align="center" gap="3">
      <Switch size="2" />
      <Text size="3">Run the migration first</Text>
    </Flex>

    <Slider defaultValue={[25]} aria-label="Rollout percentage" />

    <Flex gap="3" justify="flex-end">
      <Button emphasis="quiet" bordered>Cancel</Button>
      <Button emphasis="loud">Deploy</Button>
    </Flex>
  </Stack>
</Card>
```

The buttons sink. The switch thumb springs across its track. The slider handle follows your
pointer.

There is no duration prop, no easing prop and nothing called `animate`. Components cannot write
a duration either: every part of every transition has to resolve to a motion token, so a
hand-typed `150ms` fails the build.

## Movement overshoots once, at most

Both springs pass their target at most once. A second pass is a wobble, and a wobbling control
looks mechanical.

If you want a movement to be more noticeable, move it further. Do not loosen the spring. A
looser curve over a short distance just reads as a bouncy effect. The physics is fixed;
distance is the part a component owns.

## A focus ring cannot animate a short distance

A focus ring needs about four pixels of travel to be worth animating. Chrome rounds
`outline-offset` to whole pixels, so a ring with two pixels of room animates in three frames,
and three frames look like a stutter rather than a movement. That is why a field's focus ring
appears instantly. It is a measured limit, not a preference.

## Sometimes the movement is the information

A Spinner, and a Progress bar with no value, are a different category. Their movement is not a
transition between two states — the movement is the whole message.

When someone has asked their operating system for reduced motion, both slow down, and the
spinner slows to three seconds. Neither stops. A busy indicator that stops has lost the only
thing it was saying.

## Reduced motion stops everything else

Every other movement stops when someone asks for reduced motion. A panel is simply absent and
then present. Nothing travels, and the system does not take a measurement that only existed to
feed an animation.

A half-applied suppression is worse than none: someone who asked their computer for stillness
has to get all of it.

The stylesheet that starts a movement is the same one that switches it off, on the same
selector. So a new animation cannot arrive without its own off switch, and no specificity fight
can leave one part still moving.

## Nothing runs in JavaScript while you interact

Hover, press, focus and the validity states are all stylesheet rules reading data attributes off
the element. No handler measures anything while you are pointing at a control, and there is no
runtime animation library.

There are three exceptions, and none of them happens while you are interacting. A floating panel
measures where it is going once, when it opens. A tab bar re-measures its underline when the
selection changes. The glass effect is built when a panel mounts and when the window resizes.
