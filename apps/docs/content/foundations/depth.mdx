There is no elevation axis. No component takes a shadow, and no call site chooses one.

`depth` is a `Theme` axis with two values. It states whether this application has a light
source. Each family then decides whether it casts a shadow, and the system decides that once.

## The elevation axis is deleted

The specification once held a four-level shadow ladder: flat, raised, floating and overlay. The
team removed it after the first visual review.

The review found a larger fault than an incorrect ladder. **No call site had ever chosen an
elevation.** A Card is never floating. A Menu is never flat. A Dialog is always an overlay. A
prop that nobody varies is a component fact in the shape of an axis.

One word replaces the ladder:

```tsx
<Theme depth="elevated">
  <App />
</Theme>
```

`elevated` is the default. It states that the world has a light source. Surfaces sit above the
page, the focal control catches light, and grips lift.

`flat` states that the world has no light source. Line and fill then state every separation.

`--shadow-1` to `--shadow-5` are public tokens. They are a resource, not an axis. Components
reach them only through the world's chrome roles. A test walks every shipped stylesheet and
asserts that no rule names a shadow row directly.

For a block you own, use the visible escape:

```tsx
<Box style={{ boxShadow: "var(--shadow-3)" }}>Custom pane</Box>
```

`Box` carried a typed `shadow` prop for one day. The team removed it, because `Box` is layout
and a shadow is paint.

## The palette is ordered by reach

The palette has five rows. Row 1 is an inset well. The other four are drops.

Each drop uses the same anatomy at a different height: a contact line under the edge, which
makes the shadow read as sharp, and halos pulled in under the element by negative spread, so the
depth falls below the element instead of spreading sideways.

**A smaller element casts a smaller shadow.** A shadow that is too large makes the element read
as swollen. A card is a larger box than a menu, so the surface role uses a row with a longer
reach than the floating role.

This reverses an earlier decision. That decision said a floating pane must cast a larger shadow
than everything under it. A panel states its authority by covering what is beneath it, not by
casting a larger shadow than a card three times its size.

The relationship between size and reach is an observation. The row values are a judgment.

## What casts a shadow

These cast: `Card` and the panes that use its identity, the **loud** rung of a button, the
grips, and the key cap.

These do not cast: `Surface`, fields, text areas, the medium and quiet rungs, marks, progress
bars, separators and tab bars.

Depth separates the focal element and the floating element. Everything else sits in the plane or
on it.

Three of those refusals need a reason.

- **A ground casts nothing.** A `Surface` is what objects sit on. It is not an object. A Card
  inside it lifts. The bed it lifts off does not.
- **A field is a well.** A field is content in a plane, not a plane above one. A recessed
  element cannot cast a drop shadow, and no platform shades an input. The team reopened this
  question four times and closed it the same way each time.
- **A medium fill sits on the plane.** The team measured a medium button beside a loud button.
  Both appeared to float, and the rank stopped reading. Loud casts a shadow and catches light.
  Medium does neither.

A checked mark catches light and never casts a shadow. A mark is a well that fills, not a raised
key.

## A floating layer states its coverage

A popup covers the content under it. It must show the reader a boundary.

In `elevated`, the floating chrome shows that boundary. In `flat`, nothing casts a shadow,
including menus and dialogs. A pane then states its coverage with a hairline.

`flat` kept a faded shadow under popups for a period, because it had no other way to bound a
covering pane. The hairline replaced it, and the team retired the shadow. Two cues stating one
fact is one cue too many.

A `Dialog` casts no shadow of its own in either world. **The scrim states the separation.** The
whole viewport goes dark, which states the coverage more completely than a shadow. The panel
keeps the world's ordinary surface chrome, which is the same shadow a Card wears.

## The grips cast in both worlds

The slider grip, the switch thumb, the chosen segment of a segmented control and `Kbd` cast a
shadow in both worlds, including `flat`.

The shape states the role here, as it does for the radius exceptions. A grip that does not sit
above its rail stops reading as a grip. A grip's position is the value, so losing that reading
loses the control. Every platform ships the handle with a shadow in every context.

`Kbd` is the opposite case. A key presses into its surface, so its depth is relief rather than
lift.

"Always" describes the world, not the state. A disabled grip drops its shadow and dims. It does
not disappear, because a grey grip removes the reading instead of quieting it.

## What you cannot do

No component takes a shadow prop, an elevation prop or a `raised` boolean.

When a region inside an elevated application needs no light, nest a `Theme`:

```tsx
<Theme depth="flat" render={<section />}>
  <Card>Archived</Card>
</Theme>
```

The system gives you an app-level identity and a visible escape. It does not give you a
per-element control, because a reader perceives light as a property of the world rather than of
one element.
