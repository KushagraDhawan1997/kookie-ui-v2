You name what a colour means, and the theme calculates the value, which then changes with the
light or dark mode, the contrast setting, and the surface underneath.

The theme generates every value from two numbers per colour family, so you write no hex values
and never have to remember a palette.

## Say what you mean, not which colour

```tsx
// Do not do this.
<Text style={{ color: "#E5484D" }}>Payment failed</Text>

// Do this.
<Text tone="destructive">Payment failed</Text>
```

A hex value never changes, which gives it four problems:

- It stays the same in dark mode.
- It ignores the high-contrast setting.
- It survives a change of brand colour.
- It refuses to blend with the panel behind glass.

`tone="destructive"` solves all four, because the theme produces the value.

There's no `color` or `variant` prop, so use `style` when you must set a colour yourself, where
it stays easy to see in a review. Writing `style` for colour often means a colour family is
missing, and that family belongs in the configuration.

## The ten families

Every family reads the same lightness scale, so step 9 of red and step 9 of blue share a
lightness.

| tone | what it is for |
|---|---|
| `neutral` | Text, hairlines, wells, and all items that have no meaning of their own. This is most of an interface. |
| `accent` | Your brand. Use it at full strength one time on each screen. |
| `blue` `green` `orange` `amber` | Colour as data: a chart series, a project label, or a category. The colour *is* the information. |
| `destructive` | The action that removes something, and the error state. |
| `success` `warning` `info` | Status: the action worked, the item needs attention, or the text gives some context. |

Use a meaning name when the item carries a meaning, such as an error, and a category name when
it carries only a category, such as one series in a chart.

### Success and green are separate families

`success` and `green` resolve to the same value today while remaining two separate families,
which lets you change each meaning independently. You can move `success` towards teal and the
green in your chart stays where it is. `accent` and `blue` behave the same way, so changing your
brand to green moves `accent` and leaves `blue` alone.

Never write `tone="green"` when you mean success. The screen looks correct today and becomes
incorrect the first time either family moves.

## Components use roles, not steps

The twelve-step scale is public and you can read a step from it, whereas components read named
roles instead, which is what makes every value move together when you change a tone.

```css
--accent-soft / -soft-hover / -soft-active      /* tints */
--accent-solid / -solid-hover / -solid-active   /* fills */
--accent-border
--accent-text                                   /* links and prose on a tint */
--accent-contrast                               /* the readable text on a solid fill */
--accent-ink / -ink-muted / -ink-faint          /* the three text colours */
--accent-glyph                                  /* icons and small marks */
--accent-a1 … --accent-a12                      /* semi-transparent, blends over the surface */
```

Each family has the same set of roles. `--destructive-solid`, `--success-ink`, and
`--warning-border` all exist. One role name works for all ten families.

## Tints and borders are grey in every family

A tinted fill is almost transparent, so at 11% opacity you see mostly the page behind it and a
coloured tint carries very little information. Tinted fills are therefore **grey in every
family**, which makes a medium button, a quiet button under the pointer, and a chip grey for all
tones, while the family shows in the *text*.

Borders follow the same rule, because a pale coloured border also carries very little
information. On a button, `bordered` is a step of loudness, running quiet, then quiet with an
outline, then medium, and that order holds just as well in grey.

Checkbox borders and field borders stay where they are, because they read a grey line of their
own that never came from a family.

Solid fills keep their colour.

## The accent goes grey in two more places

Two things keep their colour in the other nine families and go grey for the accent:

- the tinted background on a panel
- the two quieter text colours

A `destructive` Notice therefore remains a box with a red tint, and a `destructive` paragraph at
medium remains a quieter red, while an `accent` Notice and an `accent` paragraph are grey.

The other nine families carry meanings, and a pale red still reads as danger. The accent carries
your brand instead, and an 11% tint of a brand colour no longer reads as that brand, which is why
the accent goes grey exactly where the meanings keep their colour.

## Icons use their own colour

Icons read `--{tone}-glyph`, which carries the family's hue at full saturation at a lightness
that keeps a small mark legible against the background of each mode.

Two simpler answers fail:

- **A fill cannot serve as an icon colour**, because it holds one value in both modes and no
  single colour stays legible on both white and black.
- **A text colour stays legible everywhere**, but the theme calculates it for reading, and that
  higher standard costs saturation. An accent icon in the text colour arrives as a muted navy
  rather than the colour you selected.

The glyph colour runs 1.3 to 2.2 times more saturated than the text colour, in every family and
in both modes.

A grey has no saturation to keep, so its glyph colour is its text colour.

## The three text colours

On text, `emphasis` selects one of three colours:

- Loud is `--color-text`.
- Medium is `--color-text-muted`.
- Quiet is `--color-text-faint`.

If you add a `tone`, the same three levels move to that family.

```tsx
<Card size="3">
  <Stack gap="3">
    <Flex gap="2" align="center" justify="space-between">
      <Heading size="6">Invoice 4021</Heading>
      <Text size="2" tone="destructive" weight="medium">Payment failed</Text>
    </Flex>
    <Text size="3">Your card was declined on 3 August.</Text>
    <Text size="3" emphasis="medium">We retry once more on 10 August.</Text>
    <Text size="2" emphasis="quiet">Refunds are not available on this plan.</Text>
    <Flex gap="3">
      <Button emphasis="loud" tone="destructive">Update card</Button>
      <Button emphasis="quiet" bordered>Contact support</Button>
    </Flex>
  </Stack>
</Card>
```

The heading and the first line are loud. The retry date carries real information that the screen
does not emphasise, which makes it medium, while the refund line states a rule nobody can act on,
which makes it quiet. One tone reaches both the small label and the button, because every family
carries the same roles.

The theme calculates each level against a contrast target, once per family and once per mode,
which gives every family the same contrast at the same level.

- **Loud answers to no target.** It stays the family's designed text colour, because loud is the
  colour the system uses most and a target number should not govern it.
- **Medium sits exactly on the minimum contrast for reading**, which is APCA Lc 60, a measured
  limit. Use it for real information that the screen does not emphasise, such as a placeholder, a
  timestamp, or a group label.
- **Quiet sits below that minimum on purpose**, at Lc 30. Use it for something the system has
  disabled, such as an option nobody can select.

Never set a full paragraph in quiet. Keep less important text loud at a smaller size, or move it
to medium.

## The high-contrast setting

`contrast="high"` is a Theme setting rather than a prop on each component, and it follows the
operating system as well, so a person who asks their system for more contrast receives it without
you doing anything.

```tsx
<Theme contrast="high">{children}</Theme>
```

A border or fill at rest is decoration, which is a judgment of taste that usually answers to no
minimum contrast, whereas under `contrast="high"` decoration must meet measured minimums. Text,
the focus ring, and the error border meet their minimums at every setting and in both modes,
because all three carry information.

The setting gives each colour as much contrast as that colour can hold, rather than adding a
fixed amount everywhere. A bright hue's border already sits at its limit, and more contrast turns
yellow into olive, so a bright hue receives its added contrast in the text instead. Parts of some
colours therefore stay where they are, which is deliberate.

## The three background colours

Three named backgrounds sit beneath everything else. From the bottom up:

```css
--color-page      /* your app's background. No component reads it. */
--color-ground    /* a bounded region that holds objects */
--color-surface   /* an object's own opaque fill */
```

<ColorGrounds />

The library paints no page background, because a Theme that painted one would repaint the page
every time you nested a Theme inside another. Paint it yourself and read `--color-page` for the
value.

`--color-ground` is the region under an object, such as a sidebar background or an area that
holds cards, while `--color-surface` is the object itself and stays opaque. Only the material
setting makes a surface transparent.

Light mode and dark mode arrange these three colours differently, which changes how you compose a
screen.

**In light mode, the page and a card share one white.** A card placed directly on the page has
no fill that sets it apart, so its shadow separates it, or its hairline does in a flat theme.
Apple's platforms behave the same way in light mode, which makes `depth` more important in light
mode than in dark. A ground sits one step darker than both the page and the card, so the ground
alone differs by colour.

**In dark mode, all three colours differ.** The page is darkest, the ground is lighter, and a
card is lightest, with each step small and deliberate. The ground carries a selected value rather
than a step of the palette, because the nearest palette step was lighter than the card and would
have made cards look darker than the region holding them.
