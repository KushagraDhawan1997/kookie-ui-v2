You name what a colour means, and the theme works out the value. The value accounts for the
current light or dark mode, the contrast setting, and the surface it sits on.

Every value is generated from two numbers per colour family. You write no hex values, and you
memorise no palette.

## Say what you mean, not which colour

```tsx
// Do not do this.
<Text style={{ color: "#E5484D" }}>Payment failed</Text>

// Do this.
<Text tone="destructive">Payment failed</Text>
```

A hex value never changes. It doesn't follow dark mode, it doesn't respond to the
high-contrast setting, it doesn't survive a rebrand, and behind glass it doesn't blend with the
panel. `tone="destructive"` handles all four, because the theme produces the value.

There's no `color` or `variant` prop. The escape is `style`, and a `style` value is easy to see
in a review. If you write `style` for colour often, a colour family is missing. Add it to the
configuration.

## The ten families

Every family is built on the same lightness scale, so step 9 of red and step 9 of blue are
equally light.

| tone | what it is for |
|---|---|
| `neutral` | Text, hairlines, wells, and anything with no meaning of its own. Most of an interface. |
| `accent` | Your brand. Use it once per screen at full strength. |
| `blue` `green` `orange` `amber` | Colour as data: a chart series, a project label, a category. The colour *is* the information. |
| `destructive` | The action that removes something, and the error state. |
| `success` `warning` `info` | Status: it worked, it needs attention, or here is some context. |

Use a meaning name when the thing has a meaning, such as an error. Use a category name when
it only has a category, such as one series in a chart.

### Success and green are separate families

`success` and `green` render the same value today, and they're still two separate families.
You can correct each meaning on its own. `success` can move toward teal without dragging your
chart's green with it. `accent` and `blue` work the same way. If you rebrand to green, you move
`accent` and leave `blue` alone.

So don't write `tone="green"` when you mean success. The screen looks right today, and it
becomes wrong the first time either family's value changes.

## Components use roles, not steps

The twelve-step scale is public, and you can read a step from it. Components never do. They
read named roles instead, so swapping a tone changes every value at once.

```css
--accent-soft / -soft-hover / -soft-active      /* tints */
--accent-solid / -solid-hover / -solid-active   /* fills */
--accent-border
--accent-text                                   /* links and prose on a tint */
--accent-contrast                               /* the readable text on a solid fill */
--accent-ink / -ink-muted / -ink-faint          /* the three text colours */
--accent-glyph                                  /* icons and small marks */
--accent-a1 … --accent-a12                      /* semi-transparent, blends over the surface */
```

Every family emits the same set. `--destructive-solid`, `--success-ink`, and
`--warning-border` all exist, and one role name works across all ten.

## Tints and borders are grey in every family

A tinted fill is mostly transparent. At 11% opacity, most of what you see is the page behind
it, so a coloured tint says very little. Tinted fills are therefore **grey in every family**. A
medium button, a hovered quiet button, and a chip are grey whatever tone you give them, and the
family shows up in the *text* instead.

Borders follow the same rule. A pale coloured border says very little, and `bordered` on a
button is a loudness step (quiet, then quiet with an outline, then medium) that works the same
in grey.

Checkbox and field borders don't change. They use a grey line of their own, which was never
the family's.

Solid fills keep their colour.

## The accent goes grey in two more places

Two things stay coloured for the other nine families and go grey for the accent: the tinted
background on a panel, and the two quieter text colours. So a `destructive` Notice is still a
red-tinted box, and a `destructive` paragraph at medium is still a quieter red, while an
`accent` one is grey.

The other nine families carry meanings, and a pale red still looks like danger. The accent
is your brand colour, and an 11% tint of it no longer looks like your brand. So the accent
stays grey where the meanings stay coloured.

## Icons use their own colour

Icons read `--{tone}-glyph`: the family's hue at full saturation, at whichever lightness makes a
small mark legible on that mode's background.

Two simpler answers don't work. A **fill** can't be an icon colour, because it's one value
in both modes, and no single colour is legible on both white and black. **Text colour** is
legible everywhere, but it's calculated for reading, and meeting that higher bar costs
saturation. An accent icon in the text colour comes out a muted navy rather than the colour
you picked. The glyph colour is between 1.3 and 2.2 times more saturated than the text colour
in every family and both modes.

A grey has no saturation to protect, so its glyph colour is its text colour.

## The three text colours

On text, `emphasis` picks one of three: loud is `--color-text`, medium is `--color-text-muted`,
quiet is `--color-text-faint`. Add a `tone` and those same three move onto that family.

```tsx
<Card size="3">
  <Stack gap="3">
    <Flex gap="2" align="center" justify="space-between">
      <Heading size="6">Invoice 4021</Heading>
      <Text size="2" tone="destructive" weight="medium">Payment failed</Text>
    </Flex>
    <Text size="3">Your card was declined on 3 August.</Text>
    <Text size="3" emphasis="medium">We retry once more on 10 August.</Text>
    <Text size="2" emphasis="quiet">Refunds are not available on this plan.</Text>
    <Flex gap="3">
      <Button emphasis="loud" tone="destructive">Update card</Button>
      <Button emphasis="quiet" bordered>Contact support</Button>
    </Flex>
  </Stack>
</Card>
```

The heading and the first line rest loud. The retry date is real information the screen
isn't emphasising, so it's medium. The refund line is a rule nobody can act on, so it's quiet.
One tone reaches both the small label and the button, because every family has the same roles.

Each level is calculated against a contrast target, once per family and once per mode, so
every family reaches the same contrast at the same level.

- **Loud isn't solved against a target.** It stays the family's own designed text colour.
  Loud is the most-used colour in the system, so a target number doesn't control it.
- **Medium sits exactly on the readability floor** (APCA Lc 60, a measured limit). Use it
  for real information the screen isn't emphasising: a placeholder, a timestamp, a group label.
- **Quiet sits below that floor on purpose** (Lc 30). Use it for something the system has
  turned off, such as an option nobody can take.

Don't set a whole paragraph in quiet. Text that matters less stays loud at a smaller size, or
moves to medium.

## The high-contrast setting

`contrast="high"` is a Theme setting, not a per-component prop. It also responds to the
operating system, so someone who has asked for more contrast gets it without you doing
anything.

```tsx
<Theme contrast="high">{children}</Theme>
```

A resting border or fill is decoration. Decoration is judged by eye, and normally no contrast
floor applies to it. Under `contrast="high"` it's held to measured floors instead. Text, the
focus ring, and the error border meet their floors at every setting, in both modes, because
those three carry information.

The setting gives each colour as much contrast as that colour can hold, rather than a fixed
increase everywhere. A bright hue's border is already at its limit, and pushing it further turns
yellow into olive, so a bright hue gets its extra contrast in the text instead. Some parts of
some colours don't move under the setting, and that's deliberate.

## The three background colours

Three named backgrounds sit under everything, from the bottom up.

```css
--color-page      /* your app's background. No component reads it. */
--color-ground    /* a bounded region that holds objects */
--color-surface   /* an object's own opaque fill */
```

The library paints no page background, because a Theme that painted one would repaint it every
time you nested another Theme. Paint it yourself and read `--color-page` for the value.

`--color-ground` is the region an object sits on: a sidebar background, or an area holding
cards. `--color-surface` is the object itself, and it's opaque. The material setting is the only
thing that makes a surface see-through.

Light mode and dark mode stack these three differently, which changes how you compose.

**In light, the page and a card are the same white.** A card sitting straight on the page has
no fill of its own to distinguish it. Its shadow separates it, or its hairline in a flat theme.
Apple's platforms work the same way in light mode, and this is why `depth` matters more in
light than in dark. A ground is a step darker than both, so it's the only one of the three that
separates by colour.

**In dark, all three differ.** The page is darkest, a ground sits above it, and a card is
lightest. Each step is small and deliberate. The ground is a hand-picked value rather than a
step off the palette, because the nearest palette step landed above the card, which would have
made cards look darker than the region holding them.
