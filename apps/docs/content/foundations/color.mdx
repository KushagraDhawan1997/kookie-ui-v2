Colour carries meaning here. You name what something means, and the theme works out the value —
against the current light or dark mode, the current contrast setting, and whatever surface it
is sitting on.

Every value in the system is generated from two numbers per colour family. You write no hex
values and you memorise no palette.

## Say what you mean, not which colour

```tsx
// Do not do this.
<Text style={{ color: "#E5484D" }}>Payment failed</Text>

// Do this.
<Text tone="destructive">Payment failed</Text>
```

A hex value never changes. It does not follow you into dark mode, it does not respond to the
high-contrast setting, it does not survive a rebrand, and behind glass it has no relationship to
the colour it is mixing into. Naming the meaning handles all four, because you said what you
meant and the theme produced the value.

There is no `color` prop and no `variant` prop. The way out is `style`, which is easy to spot in
a review. If you find yourself writing `style` more than occasionally, a colour family is
missing — add it to the configuration.

## The ten families

Every family is built on the same lightness scale, so step 9 of red and step 9 of blue read as
the same step.

| tone | what it is for |
|---|---|
| `neutral` | Text, hairlines, wells and anything with no meaning of its own. Most of an interface. |
| `accent` | Your brand. Use it once per screen at full strength. |
| `blue` `green` `orange` `amber` | Colour as data: a chart series, a project label, a category. The colour *is* the information. |
| `destructive` | The action that removes something, and the error state. |
| `success` `warning` `info` | Status: it worked, it needs attention, or here is some context. |

Use a meaning name when the thing has a meaning. Use a category name when it only has a
category.

### `success` is not a nickname for `green`

They render the same value today, and they are still two separate families. A name carries a
meaning, and each meaning has to stay correctable on its own: `success` can move toward teal
without dragging your chart's green with it. `accent` and `blue` are the same story — someone
who rebrands to green moves `accent` and leaves `blue` alone.

So do not write `tone="green"` when you mean success. The screen looks right today and becomes
wrong the first time anyone re-judges either name.

## Components use roles, not steps

The twelve-step scale is public and you can read a step from it. Components never do. They read
named roles instead, and that is what makes swapping a tone free.

```css
--accent-soft / -soft-hover / -soft-active      /* tints */
--accent-solid / -solid-hover / -solid-active   /* fills */
--accent-border
--accent-text                                   /* links and prose on a tint */
--accent-contrast                               /* the readable text on a solid fill */
--accent-ink / -ink-muted / -ink-faint          /* the three text colours */
--accent-glyph                                  /* icons and small marks */
--accent-a1 … --accent-a12                      /* semi-transparent, blends over the surface */
```

Every family emits the same set, so `--destructive-solid`, `--success-ink` and
`--warning-border` all exist and one role name works across all ten.

## Tints and borders are grey in every family

A tinted fill spends colour to say almost nothing: at 11% opacity, most of what reaches your eye
is the page. So tinted fills are **grey in every family**. A medium button, a hovered quiet
button and a badge are grey whatever tone you gave them, and the family shows up in the *text*
instead.

Borders follow for the same reason. A pale coloured border says little, and `bordered` on a
button is a loudness step — quiet, then quiet with an outline, then medium — which reads exactly
the same in grey.

Checkbox and field borders are unaffected. They have their own solved grey line and never used
the family's.

Solid fills keep their colour. That is the step this rule exists to protect.

## The accent goes grey in two more places

Two things stay coloured for the other nine families and go grey for the accent: the tinted
background on a panel, and the two quieter text colours. So a `destructive` Notice is still a
red-tinted box, and a `destructive` paragraph at medium is still a quieter red, while an
`accent` one is grey.

The reason is what an accent is. The other nine are meanings, and a pale red still reads as
danger. The accent is the colour you chose, and diluting an identity destroys it — 11% of your
brand is not your brand.

## Icons use their own colour

Icons read `--{tone}-glyph`: the family's hue at full saturation, at whichever lightness makes a
small mark legible on that mode's background.

It exists because neither obvious answer works. A **fill** cannot be an icon colour, because it
is one value in both modes and no single colour is legible on both white and black. **Text
colour** is legible everywhere but is solved for reading, and meeting that higher bar costs
saturation — an accent icon in the text colour comes out a muted navy rather than the colour you
picked. The glyph colour is between 1.3 and 2.2 times more saturated than the text colour in
every family and both modes.

A grey has no saturation to protect, so its glyph colour is its text colour.

## The three text colours

On text, `emphasis` picks one of three: loud is `--color-text`, medium is `--color-text-muted`,
quiet is `--color-text-faint`. Add a `tone` and those same three move onto that family.

```tsx
<Card size="3">
  <Stack gap="3">
    <Flex gap="2" align="center" justify="space-between">
      <Heading size="6">Invoice 4021</Heading>
      <Text size="2" tone="destructive" weight="medium">Payment failed</Text>
    </Flex>
    <Text size="3">Your card was declined on 3 August.</Text>
    <Text size="3" emphasis="medium">We retry once more on 10 August.</Text>
    <Text size="2" emphasis="quiet">Refunds are not available on this plan.</Text>
    <Flex gap="3">
      <Button emphasis="loud" tone="destructive">Update card</Button>
      <Button emphasis="quiet" bordered>Contact support</Button>
    </Flex>
  </Stack>
</Card>
```

The heading and the first line rest loud. The retry date is real information the screen is not
emphasising, so it is medium. The refund line is a rule nobody can act on, so it is quiet. One
tone reaches both the small label and the button, because every family has the same roles.

Each level is solved against a contrast target, once per family and once per mode, so every
family reaches the same contrast at the same level. Three things are worth knowing:

- **Loud is not solved.** It stays the family's own designed text colour. This is a judgment:
  it is the colour the system uses more than any other, so a target number should not be in
  charge of it.
- **Medium sits exactly on the readability floor** (APCA Lc 60, a measured limit). Use it for
  real information the screen is not emphasising: a placeholder, a timestamp, a group label.
- **Quiet sits below that floor on purpose** (Lc 30). Use it for something the system has turned
  off, such as an option nobody can take. It is a written exception, not an oversight.

Do not set a whole paragraph in quiet. Text that matters less stays loud at a smaller size, or
moves to medium.

## The high-contrast setting

`contrast="high"` is a Theme setting, not a per-component prop. It also responds to the
operating system, so someone who has asked for more contrast gets it without you doing
anything.

```tsx
<Theme contrast="high">{children}</Theme>
```

A resting border or fill is decoration. Those are judged by eye and held to no contrast floor
normally. Under `contrast="high"` they are held to measured floors instead. Text, the focus
ring and the error border always meet their floors, in both modes, because those three are
signals — and a signal that only works when someone opts in is not a signal.

The setting gives each colour as much contrast as that colour can hold, rather than a fixed
increase everywhere. A bright hue's border is already at its limit, and pushing it further turns
yellow into olive, so a bright hue gets its extra contrast in the text instead. If one part of a
colour does not move under this setting, that is the right answer for that part.

## The three background colours

Three named backgrounds sit under everything, from the bottom up.

```css
--color-page      /* your app's background. No component reads it. */
--color-ground    /* a bounded region that holds objects */
--color-surface   /* an object's own opaque fill */
```

The library paints no page background, because a Theme that painted one would repaint it every
time you nested another Theme. Paint it yourself and read `--color-page` for the value.

`--color-ground` is the region an object sits on: a sidebar background, or an area holding
cards. `--color-surface` is the object itself, and it is opaque. The material setting is the
only thing that makes a surface see-through.

The two modes stack these three differently, and it changes how you compose.

**In light, the page and a card are the same white.** A card sitting straight on the page has no
fill of its own to distinguish it, so what separates it is its shadow — or its hairline, in a
flat theme. That is how light mode works on Apple's platforms too, and it is why `depth` carries
more weight in light than you might expect. A ground is a step darker than both, so of the three
it is the only one that separates by colour.

**In dark, all three differ.** The page is darkest, a ground sits above it, and a card is
lightest. Each step is small and deliberate: the ground is a hand-picked value rather than a
step off the palette, because the nearest palette step landed above the card and would have made
cards look darker than the region holding them.
